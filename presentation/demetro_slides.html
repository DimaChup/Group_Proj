SLIDE 1 — VISION (1.6 min, 246 words)
[After Edward finishes] So Edward showed you how we trained the model — now let me walk you through what actually happens when it runs on the drone. [Point to pipeline] The Pi camera captures frames at about 5 FPS. Each frame goes through our YOLOv8 model running on TFLite, and when we get a detection, we convert that pixel position into a real-world GPS estimate using the drone's altitude and field of view. [Point to tilt boxes] Now, one problem we hit early was attitude error. If the drone is tilted even a few degrees during flight, your GPS estimate drifts significantly — we measured up to 6.2 metres off. So we added attitude compensation using the IMU pitch and roll data, which brought that error down to about 0.3 metres. [Point to four phases] But a single estimate is still noisy, so we use a four-phase approach. Multiple detections get clustered and averaged, bringing our estimate from about 3.5 metres down to 1.8. And then — this is the key insight — once we centre the drone directly above the target, the drone's own GPS becomes our target GPS. No more pixel maths needed. [Point to video / dashboard] As you can see in the simulation video, the operator gets a live browser dashboard showing detections, the GPS estimate, and simple Y, N, or I buttons to confirm or reject each target. Worth noting — the same codebase runs on both the Pi with TFLite and our laptops with Ultralytics, so we can test everything in simulation before it ever touches real hardware.
SLIDE 2 — LADDER (1.5 min, 219 words)
[Slide appears] Our testing philosophy was simple: never skip a rung. We built a four-level progressive testing ladder, where each level had to pass before we moved to the next. [Point to bottom rung] At the base, full software-in-the-loop simulation. The same Python code that runs on the Raspberry Pi runs identically on a laptop with a simulated drone. No separate sim version — one codebase throughout. In simulation, we verified all twelve mission requirements, backed by fifty-eight test scripts and a hundred and twenty-seven unit tests. [Move up] Level two: Pi bench testing. Camera capturing frames, AI model running inference, flight controller connected and responding to commands — all verified on the hardware, on the bench, before anything left the ground. [Next rung] Level three: Easter field testing. We took the system outdoors, calibrated the camera's field of view with a real dummy at measured distances, verified GPS accuracy, and confirmed detection performance under natural lighting conditions. [Top rung] Level four is today — real autonomous flight. [Pause] What made this work is that we started building simulation early, before we had hardware access. That constraint became our greatest strength. By the time the drone was physically assembled at Easter, the software was already solid. Over four thousand lines of flight code, tested at every level. [Hand off] Now I'll hand over to Herish, who'll walk you through the personal locator beacon integration.
