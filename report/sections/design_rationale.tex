\section{Design Rationale}\label{sec:rationale}

The mission scenario and requirements (Section~\ref{sec:intro-d6}) demand a low-cost, fully autonomous SAR platform that searches a constrained area, detects a casualty, and lands nearby---all without a continuous data link. This section justifies the design decisions that translate those requirements into an implementable system, beginning with a stakeholder-level STEEPLE assessment and then presenting the trade studies that determined operating parameters, hardware platform, software architecture, and mission control strategy.

\subsection{Search Parameter Optimisation}\label{sec:search-param-opt}

Three coupled decision variables---altitude~$h$, scan angle~$\theta$, and speed~$v$---govern the search pattern and jointly determine five competing objectives: coverage completeness, detection probability, mission time, energy consumption, and NFZ safety margin.

These objectives cannot be optimised independently. Lowering altitude improves detection confidence but narrows the camera footprint and increases scan lines. Increasing speed reduces mission time but shortens target dwell time and lowers detection probability.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{coupling_matrix}
  \caption{Variable coupling matrix. The altitude--speed pair exhibits the dominant interaction, jointly determining coverage rate and detection probability. Off-diagonal cells identify which performance metric each variable pair affects.}
  \label{fig:coupling-body}
\end{figure}

A 216-configuration parametric sweep (6~altitudes $\times$ 36~scan angles, Appendix~\ref{sec:path-opt}) evaluated every combination on the AENGM0074 polygon using a physics-based energy model. Scan angle dominates altitude for energy efficiency: varying the angle from optimal (\SI{70}{\degree}) to worst-case (\SI{0}{\degree}) increases energy by 100--200\%, whereas changing altitude from \SI{35}{\metre} to \SI{50}{\metre} saves only 30--40\%. A dependency chain links each parameter to a physical measurement or constraint:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Target $\rightarrow$ altitude}: the mannequin's \SI{0.5}{\metre} width must subtend $\geq$10~model-input pixels for reliable detection, capping altitude at \SI{35}{\metre} with a $1.4\times$ margin.
  \item \textbf{Altitude $\rightarrow$ speed}: the \SI{24}{\metre} along-track footprint at \SI{35}{\metre}, combined with \SI{4.8}{FPS} inference, requires $\geq$10~frames per target, yielding $v \leq \SI{11.5}{\metre\per\second}$; the altitude-dependent speed schedule assigns $v = \SI{8}{\metre\per\second}$.
  \item \textbf{Polygon geometry $\rightarrow$ scan angle}: aligning with the longest edge (\SI{70}{\degree}) minimises U-turns from 9 to 5 and reduces energy from \SI{25.4}{Wh} to \SI{12.6}{Wh}.
  \item \textbf{Footprint width $\rightarrow$ NFZ margin}: the \SI{32}{\metre} footprint at \SI{35}{\metre} requires \SI{23}{\metre} Root Sum of Squares (RSS) standoff; the \SI{30}{\metre} buffer provides a $2.2\times$ safety factor.
\end{enumerate}

  \caption{Altitude--speed trade-off space. Colour encodes composite mission score (40\% detection $+$ 35\% coverage $+$ 25\% energy). The selected operating point (\SI{35}{\metre}, \SI{8}{\metre\per\second}) sits above the 95\% detection contour in the high-score region, trading \SI{4.4}{Wh} of energy (10.9\% of battery) for a comfortable detection margin.}
  \label{fig:alt-speed-body}

\noindent The selected operating point (\SI{35}{\metre}, \SI{70}{\degree}, \SI{8}{\metre\per\second}) is not the energy minimum---that belongs to \SI{50}{\metre}/\SI{70}{\degree}/\SI{10}{\metre\per\second} at \SI{8.2}{Wh}---but it offers the best Pareto compromise: 96\% coverage, $P_d > 99.97\%$ (assuming frame independence; 95\% per pass after correlation adjustment, see Section~\ref{app:req-detail}), \SI{12.6}{Wh} (10.9\% of battery), and a \SI{50}{\metre} NFZ margin with $2.2\times$ surplus. The 4\% coverage gap lies within a fenced wildlife reserve with negligible casualty probability. Appendices~\ref{sec:path-opt} and~\ref{sec:design-strategy} provide the full derivation, energy model, strategy comparison, and sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Hardware Platform Selection}\label{sec:hw-rationale}

The hardware combines a Hexsoon EDU-450 airframe, CubePilot Orange+ flight controller with ArduPilot firmware, Raspberry Pi~5 companion computer, and IMX296 global shutter camera.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global shutter camera.} The IMX296 was selected over cheaper rolling-shutter alternatives because rolling-shutter distortion at up to \SI{10}{\metre\per\second} forward flight produces ${\approx}$7.5\,px parallelogram skew per frame (Section~\ref{sec:camera-rationale}), shifting detected centroids by up to \SI{0.5}{\metre}---a systematic error that cannot be removed by averaging. The CSI interface leaves USB ports free for telemetry radios.
    \item \textbf{No thermal imaging.} Thermal cameras (e.g.\ FLIR Boson, DJI Zenmuse H20T) add \pounds2\,000--\pounds10\,000, have lower spatial resolution (typically $640 \times 512$ vs $1456 \times 1088$ RGB), and require radiometric calibration that varies with ambient temperature. Thermal imaging offers superior night-time and foliage-penetration capability, but CAA VLOS requirements restrict operations to daylight~\cite{caa2024uas}, and this project deliberately explores RGB-only detection feasibility at the low-cost extreme~\cite{thermal_sar}. A fused RGB+thermal system would suit operational deployment but exceeds the \pounds565 budget by $4$--$18\times$.
    \item \textbf{Onboard vs offboard inference.} Offloading detection to a ground station via video downlink was rejected for three reasons: (1)~reliable video streaming at \SI{1456}{} $\times$ \SI{1088}{} at 5\,FPS requires ${\approx}$\SI{15}{Mbps} sustained throughput, exceeding typical \SI{2.4}{\giga\hertz} telemetry radio capacity; (2)~round-trip latency (encode, transmit, detect, return command) adds 200--500\,ms to the decision loop, during which the drone travels 1--4\,m; and (3)~link loss during detection would leave the drone without situational awareness. Onboard inference eliminates all three failure modes at the cost of lower computational headroom.
    \item \textbf{MAVProxy bridge.} A Python~3.13 incompatibility with pyserial (dropped bytes causing \texttt{BAD\_DATA} errors) is resolved by interposing MAVProxy as a UDP bridge, which reads the serial port reliably using its own C-level handler~\cite{mavproxy}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Software Architecture Rationale}\label{sec:arch-rationale}

Two architectures were considered: (1)~a monolithic script with platform-specific branches, typical of rapid prototypes, and (2)~a modular pipeline with narrow interfaces between independent modules. The monolithic approach was rejected because adding platform support (laptop~$\rightarrow$~Pi) or swapping inference backends would require editing detection, navigation, and mission logic in a single file---a maintenance burden that grows quadratically with the number of platform$\times$feature combinations.

\noindent The selected modular architecture enforces four principles:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Modular separation}---each concern hides behind a narrow interface (e.g.\ \texttt{detect\_in\_image(frame)} $\to$ \texttt{(found, x, y, conf)}). Swapping the detection model requires zero changes to the 754-line mission orchestrator.
  \item \textbf{Platform-agnostic code}---a \texttt{MODE} flag selects the camera source; auto-detection selects the flight controller. The same \texttt{main.py} runs on laptop (SITL + webcam) and Pi (Cube + CSI camera) without conditional branches.
  \item \textbf{Configuration-driven tuning}---all 23~tunable parameters reside in \texttt{config.py}, so transitioning to real hardware requires only parameter adjustment, not code changes.
  \item \textbf{Dual inference backend}---the system auto-detects Ultralytics (laptop) or TFLite/NCNN (Pi) at import time, both producing identical $[1, 5, 8400]$ output tensors. This was validated by running the same 50-frame benchmark on both backends and confirming identical detection outputs (bounding box coordinates match to $<$0.01\,px).
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The resulting six-module decomposition (vision, planning, navigation, state machine, configuration, utilities) achieves a modularity score of 3.8/5.0 (Appendix~\ref{sec:evaluation}), with the vision module scoring 4.5/5.0 due to its single-function interface and complete backend encapsulation.

\noindent YOLOv8n is the only variant that achieves real-time inference on the Pi~5 CPU within the 300\,ms-per-frame budget required at search speed. YOLOv8s (11.2\,M parameters) and YOLOv8m (25.9\,M) exceed this budget by $2\times$ and $4\times$, respectively. SSD~MobileNetV2 matches YOLOv8n on speed but delivers lower accuracy on COCO~\cite{lin2014coco}. Note that the SSD mAP of 0.88 is measured on COCO whereas the YOLO figures use the custom dataset, a fair single-class SSD comparison would require retraining on the same data. Critically, SSD~MobileNetV2 small-object mAP drops to ${\sim}$0.22 on COCO~\cite{edgeai}, directly relevant at the detection ceiling where the target subtends ${\sim}$20\,px.

The model was trained as a single-class detector on 366 images (300 synthetic, 16 real, 50 negatives) at native camera resolution ($1456 \times 1088$), following the domain-randomisation principle~\cite{domainrand}. Training at higher resolution than the $640 \times 640$ inference input lets the network learn fine-grained features that survive downscaling~\cite{yolov8}. A COCO-pretrained person detector serves as a fallback for field conditions where the custom model underperforms.

\paragraph{Why TFLite over other inference runtimes?} Three alternative runtimes were considered: ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, and NCNN. TensorRT requires an NVIDIA GPU (absent on the Pi~5). ONNX Runtime supports ARM CPU but its Python wheel for aarch64 lags behind mainline releases and benchmarked 15--20\% slower than TFLite with XNNPACK on the Pi~5. NCNN (Tencent) achieved $4.5\times$ faster inference than TFLite in benchmarks (\SI{72}{\milli\second} vs \SI{327}{\milli\second} full pipeline) and is supported as a secondary backend; however, its Python bindings are less mature, and the TFLite ecosystem provides the most robust export path from Ultralytics. The dual-backend architecture (TFLite primary, NCNN secondary) preserves the option to switch at deployment time via a single command-line flag.

\subsection{State Machine Design}\label{sec:fsm-rationale}

A deterministic finite state machine (FSM) with 20~states and 32~transitions was chosen over behaviour trees and reactive architectures for its verifiability, traceability, and ease of runtime monitoring~\cite{wagner2006fsm}.

\paragraph{Why not behaviour trees?} Behaviour trees offer superior composability for complex agent behaviours and are widely used in game AI and multi-robot systems~\cite{colosseum2017bt}. However, they introduce implicit priority ordering through selector/sequence nodes that is harder to audit than an explicit transition table, debugging requires tree-visualisation tooling not available on a headless Pi, and the SAR mission's strictly sequential phase structure (search $\rightarrow$ detect $\rightarrow$ verify $\rightarrow$ land) does not benefit from the parallel subtree execution that behaviour trees enable. Quantitatively, the mission graph contains zero concurrent branches---every state has exactly one active successor---so the $O(n\log n)$ tick traversal of a behaviour tree reduces to the FSM's $O(1)$ dictionary dispatch with no functional benefit. For a single-drone, single-objective mission, the FSM's one-state-at-a-time guarantee makes every transition auditable in a flat log file.

\paragraph{Why not reactive/subsumption architectures?} Reactive architectures (e.g.\ Brooks' subsumption~\cite{brooks1986subsumption}) excel at low-latency obstacle avoidance but lack the persistent state memory required for multi-phase missions: the system must remember which of the 5--9 search legs have been completed, which of potentially multiple targets have been classified (Y/N/I/X), and how long the current hover has lasted (for the \SI{120}{\second} timeout). Layering these concerns onto a purely reactive stack would replicate FSM functionality without its formal verifiability.

\noindent Each state maps to a single handler function via a dictionary-based dispatch table ($O(1)$ lookup), eliminating fall-through errors. Defence-in-depth safety is enforced through five independent mechanisms: manual override (M~key), RC kill switch (hardware-level), return-to-launch on link loss, five-layer geofence, and per-state timeouts. Every blocking state carries a wall-clock timeout to prevent indefinite hangs. The complete transition table is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:states}.

The CENTERING state---hovering stationary above a detected target before estimating its GPS position---simultaneously eliminates roll/pitch projection error (\SI{6.2}{\metre} during flight to $<$\SI{0.6}{\metre}), motion blur, and GPS timing lag. This single procedural step replaces three independent software corrections (attitude compensation, lag prediction, and blur deconvolution), each of which would add complexity and potential failure modes to a safety-critical code path. Appendix~\ref{sec:centering-mitigation} provides the full quantitative analysis.

\subsection{Autonomy Level Justification}\label{sec:autonomy-rationale}

The system operates at Sheridan Level~7--8 (``computer executes autonomously, human monitors'')~\cite{sheridan2002humans} for the search phase and Level~3--4 (``computer suggests one alternative, human approves'') for the landing decision. This hybrid was chosen after analysing the asymmetric cost structure of SAR false positives versus false negatives~\cite{endsley1999loa, cummings2014man_uav}.

A \emph{false-positive landing} (descending on a rock, tree stump, or discarded clothing) incurs three compounding penalties: (1)~battery endurance consumed by descent, hover, and re-climb (${\approx}\SI{90}{\second}$, roughly 8\% of a 20-minute endurance budget); (2)~search coverage paused during the entire descent--verify--climb cycle; and (3)~risk of mechanical damage on uneven terrain. In a single-drone mission with no redundancy, even one unnecessary landing significantly reduces the probability of completing the search before battery exhaustion.

A \emph{false negative} (failing to investigate a real casualty) is worse in absolute terms but is mitigated by the lawnmower pattern's guarantee of revisiting every cell: a missed detection on pass~1 can be recovered on the return leg. The operator-in-the-loop gate therefore addresses the higher-consequence, lower-recoverable error (committing to a landing) while the autonomous search handles the high-bandwidth, lower-consequence task (scanning terrain at 8\,m/s).

The \textsc{Verify} state implements this gate: the operator classifies each detection as confirmed~(Y), rejected~(N), or item of interest~(I) via the ground station. A \SI{120}{\second} timeout auto-rejects unresponded detections---derived from the energy budget (\SI{6.0}{Wh} per hover event, 5.2\% of battery, permitting two timeouts while retaining 89\% for search)~\cite{goodrich2007human}. Full autonomy (Level~10) was rejected because the single-class detector cannot distinguish a casualty from visually similar objects~\cite{murphy2014, lyu2023uav_sar_survey}. Manual-only operation was also rejected: human operators cannot sustain visual scanning at \SI{8}{\metre\per\second}, and automation provides a ${\sim}20\times$ coverage-rate advantage~\cite{goodrich2008}.

\subsection{Decisions That Changed: Evidence-Based Corrections}\label{sec:decisions-changed}

Seven design decisions were revised when empirical evidence contradicted initial assumptions. Each followed the same pattern: observe anomaly, diagnose root cause, implement fix, verify improvement.

\paragraph{1. Focal length: \SI{7.0}{\milli\metre} $\rightarrow$ \SI{5.46}{\milli\metre}.}
\emph{Original decision:} use the datasheet focal length of \SI{7.0}{\milli\metre} for GPS estimation.
\emph{Evidence:} a tape-measure calibration on field day measured \SI{92}{\centi\metre} visible at \SI{1}{\metre} height---22\% wider than predicted.
\emph{Revised decision:} back-calculate focal length from the physical measurement: $f = w_s \times d / W_{\text{visible}} = 5.02 \times 1.0 / 0.92 = \SI{5.46}{\milli\metre}$.
\emph{Outcome:} GPS estimation error at \SI{30}{\metre} reduced by up to \SI{3}{\metre}; without this correction, landing errors would have exceeded the \SI{10}{\metre} R07 requirement. All downstream parameters (footprint width, lane spacing, NFZ buffer) were recalculated from the corrected value.

\paragraph{2. Camera colour space: RGB assumed $\rightarrow$ BGR confirmed.}
\emph{Original decision:} apply \texttt{cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR\_RGB2BGR)} because \texttt{picamera2} labels the stream as \texttt{RGB888}.
\emph{Evidence:} detection confidence on the Pi was anomalously low ($<$0.3) despite identical model performance on the laptop. Systematic testing of all six channel permutations (RGB, RBG, GBR, GRB, BRG, BGR) revealed that the IMX296 sensor outputs BGR data despite the RGB888 label---the \texttt{cvtColor} call was inverting the channels.
\emph{Revised decision:} remove the erroneous conversion; pass raw frames directly to the detector.
\emph{Outcome:} detection confidence recovered to 0.96+ on the Pi, matching laptop performance. This bug would have caused complete detection failure in flight.

\paragraph{3. Camera resolution: $640 \times 480$ $\rightarrow$ $1456 \times 1088$.}
\emph{Original decision:} capture at $640 \times 480$ (common webcam default) to minimise processing load.
\emph{Evidence:} at $640 \times 480$ and \SI{30}{\metre} altitude, a prone casualty subtends only $20 \times 14$\,px---at the minimum detection threshold with no safety margin.
\emph{Revised decision:} capture at the IMX296's native resolution ($1456 \times 1088$), since the model resizes internally to $640 \times 640$ regardless.
\emph{Outcome:} target subtends $46 \times 32$\,px ($2.3\times$ improvement) with zero inference time penalty, providing a comfortable $1.6\times$ margin above the 20\,px detection floor.

\paragraph{4. TFLite bounding box interpretation: normalised $\rightarrow$ pixel coordinates.}
\emph{Original decision:} treat TFLite output coordinates as normalised values in $[0, 1]$ and multiply by frame dimensions, following the standard TFLite convention.
\emph{Evidence:} during Pi bench testing, detection overlay boxes were drawn off-screen (coordinates exceeding 640). Inspection revealed the TFLite backend was returning raw pixel coordinates in $[0, 640]$, not normalised values---an undocumented deviation from the expected convention.
\emph{Revised decision:} add a coordinate-range check: if any output coordinate exceeds 1.0, treat as pixel coordinates and skip the normalisation multiply. The same guard was already present in the NCNN inference path.
\emph{Outcome:} detection overlays rendered correctly; GPS estimation accuracy improved because bounding box centres were no longer scaled by an extra factor of 640. This also fixed a downstream double-scaling bug in \texttt{passive\_watch.py} where pixel coordinates were multiplied by frame dimensions a second time, placing GPS estimates over \SI{100}{\metre} from the true position.

\paragraph{5. Geofence repulsive force: sign inversion corrected.}
\emph{Original decision:} the repulsive offset function returns positive lat/lon deltas to push the drone away from the NFZ boundary.
\emph{Evidence:} SITL simulation showed the drone being pushed \emph{toward} the SSSI boundary instead of away. Root cause: \texttt{cv2.pointPolygonTest} returns positive values for points \emph{inside} a polygon (opposite to the assumed convention), and the repulsive offset signs were not negated.
\emph{Revised decision:} negate the repulsive offset returns and correct the inside/outside boundary logic.
\emph{Outcome:} geofence now correctly repels the drone from the NFZ. This defect would have caused a Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 violation in flight~\cite{wca1981}, and was caught at Tier~1 (simulation) at zero hardware cost.

\paragraph{6. TFLite runtime: \texttt{tflite-runtime} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ai-edge-litert}.}
\emph{Original decision:} use Google's \texttt{tflite-runtime} package for edge inference on the Pi.
\emph{Evidence:} \texttt{tflite-runtime} has no Python~3.13 wheel; \texttt{picamera2} requires system Python~3.13 on the Pi~5, creating an unresolvable dependency conflict.
\emph{Revised decision:} switch to \texttt{ai-edge-litert}, which provides an identical API with Python~3.13 support.
\emph{Outcome:} dependency conflict eliminated with zero code changes; verified by 50-run benchmark producing identical inference times (\SI{206.5}{\milli\second}).

\paragraph{7. Serial communication: pyserial $\rightarrow$ MAVProxy UDP bridge.}
\emph{Original decision:} connect the Pi directly to the Cube flight controller via pyserial at 921\,600~baud.
\emph{Evidence:} a Python~3.13 pyserial regression dropped bytes, producing persistent \texttt{BAD\_DATA} errors that prevented reliable MAVLink communication.
\emph{Revised decision:} interpose MAVProxy as a UDP bridge; it reads the serial port with its C-level handler and forwards to UDP endpoints.
\emph{Outcome:} reliable communication restored; MAVProxy simultaneously enables multi-consumer access (scripts on port~14550, Mission Planner on TCP~5762, diagnostics on port~14551), turning a workaround into an architectural improvement (Table~\ref{tab:mcda-comms}).

\subsection{Why Not ROS}\label{sec:ros-rationale}

ROS~2 was excluded for three reasons~\cite{quigley2009ros}: (1)~architectural mismatch---the system is a single-node pipeline (one camera, one detector, one flight controller) with 4~inter-module calls per loop iteration; ROS's pub/sub model adds serialisation and inter-process communication overhead (typically 0.5--2\,ms per message hop) without enabling new capability; (2)~deployment complexity---a minimal ROS~2 Humble installation requires ${\approx}$\SI{2}{\giga\byte} disk and 15+ system packages versus four \texttt{pip} packages totalling $<$\SI{50}{\mega\byte}, reducing SD card provisioning from ${\sim}$45\,min to $<$3\,min; and (3)~timeline---a 40--60\,hr learning curve per developer would have consumed 25\% of available development time on middleware rather than mission logic, with no proportionate risk reduction for a single-vehicle system. ROS remains recommended for future multi-drone extensions where its discovery, time-synchronisation, and launch infrastructure would provide genuine benefit.

\subsection{Camera Selection: Global vs Rolling Shutter}\label{sec:camera-rationale}

The IMX296 global shutter camera (provided as university equipment) was validated for aerial search~\cite{imx296, sukhatme2012rolling_global}. At \SI{5}{\metre\per\second} and \SI{30}{\metre} altitude, ground motion across the sensor reaches ${\approx}$250\,px/s; a rolling-shutter sensor would produce ${\approx}$7.5\,px parallelogram distortion per frame, shifting detected centroids by up to \SI{0.5}{\metre}. The IMX296's global shutter eliminates this error. The trade-off---lower light sensitivity from the \SI{3.45}{\micro\metre} pixel pitch---is acceptable given daylight-only CAA VLOS requirements~\cite{caa2024uas}. During integration, the IMX296 was found to output BGR despite its RGB888 labelling; the fix was simply removing the erroneous \texttt{cv2.cvtColor()} call (Section~\ref{sec:decisions-changed}).

\subsection{Field Day Adaptation Under Uncertainty}\label{sec:weather-rationale}

The scheduled flight test (2026-03-11) was cancelled due to sustained winds exceeding the EDU-450's \SI{10}{\metre\per\second} operational limit (measured \SI{12}{\metre\per\second}, gusts to \SI{18}{\metre\per\second}). The team executed a pre-planned bench-testing fallback instead. This session yielded the FOV calibration correction (\SI{7.0}{\milli\metre}~$\rightarrow$~\SI{5.46}{\milli\metre}), lens distortion calibration (RMS~0.399), end-to-end connectivity verification, and the inference benchmark (\SI{207}{\milli\second}, 4.8\,FPS). These results are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:decisions-changed} and the evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). The go/no-go decision framework and full bench protocol are documented in Appendix~\ref{sec:field-day}.
