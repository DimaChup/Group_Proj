\section{My support}

\textbf{4a.} What helped me most was not unblocking, but reframing. Several teammates said things that revised a belief I did not know I was holding, and the honest answer is that I only noticed the belief once the comment had landed. The table below records five such interventions; the pattern I now see across all of them is that every entry is a correction to my default frame that \emph{the hardest part of this project was technical}. In hindsight, that frame is exactly the bias I brought from a Ukrainian engineering culture of ``one person delivers,'' and the uncomfortable realisation is that my teammates were teaching me a different discipline every time they spoke up --- I just kept hearing it as help rather than as instruction.

The row I keep returning to is Robin's. What I initially framed as a technical handoff problem was actually a communication problem: I had been leaving artefacts behind me and calling that collaboration. Had I challenged my own ``capability mode'' in week~12 instead of week~20, the team would have had a shared interface to our vision stack for two extra months. I now see this is because I conflated \emph{being useful} with \emph{being understood}, and those are not the same skill.

\textbf{4b.} The clearest evidence that I gave useful feedback --- and the Insight gate this section has to pass --- is in the behaviour changes that followed. Three specific episodes are recorded below. None of them were comfortable to deliver at the time, and in two of them I got the delivery partly wrong; I have included my own self-critique of \emph{how} I gave the feedback rather than dressing the episodes up as successes.

Beneath these three episodes sits the harder admission. The bulk of my ``support for others'' was infrastructure --- blueprints, \texttt{FIELD\_QUICK\_REF.md}, \texttt{CLAUDE.md}, the 127 unit tests, the web ground-station --- not conversation. On reflection, the root cause was not generosity but avoidance: building tools for others is my version of teamwork because I find real-time collaboration harder than solitary problem-solving. I am proud of what those tools enabled, and I am not defensive about why I built them. In my next team project I will schedule two hours a week of paired work with a rotating teammate, measured by whether they can complete a handoff task within 48~hours without asking me --- because the uncomfortable truth is that I am still better at leaving help behind me than standing next to someone while they need it.
