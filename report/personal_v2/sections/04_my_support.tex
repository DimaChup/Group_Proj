The non-technical help mattered as much as the technical. The project manager booking field slots while I built code meant that when we arrived at flight day 1 on 12~March we had a trained model, working simulator, progressive test plan, and printed checklists, instead of a half-finished stack and a bag of paperwork. I did not appreciate that division of labour in wk14; I did by wk20. I now see this is because I had been implicitly equating ``contribution'' with ``lines of code'' -- a criterion the simulator itself helped dismantle, producing more value per hour than any other artefact without changing any physical world state at all.

\textit{Evaluating the communication methods I used.} \rtag{M17.d} The methods I reached for were: (1)~Markdown prose, (2)~code contracts (JSON schema + CLI examples), (3)~running executable demos (20-line MAVLink heartbeat, Robin integration script), (4)~a browser GUI for the pilot, (5)~HTML slides with a teleprompter sidebar for Demetro's FDR, (6)~a formal complaint letter, and (7)~face-to-face bench walkthroughs for the pilot and project manager. My criterion for effectiveness, per recipient: did their behaviour change as I intended without a follow-up conversation? Judgement: the browser GUI and the JSON contract scored highest (zero follow-ups); the HTML slides scored high on a narrow target (Demetro's eye contact improved); Markdown prose scored lowest, because every teammate who read it had follow-ups and the project manager told me it was too dense. The comparison worth recording is that the same underlying content -- ``how to run the drone detection pipeline'' -- produced a usable artefact only when I re-cast it as a GUI plus a JSON contract instead of a Markdown document. The lesson: for engineering teammates a runnable contract beats prose; for non-engineering teammates a visual or physical artefact beats both. Default next time: \textit{what is the smallest runnable thing I could hand over?}

\textit{The non-technical audience, named explicitly.} The pilot and the project manager are the two non-technical audiences on the project -- the pilot is a licensed operator, not an engineer; the project manager coordinates logistics and does not read code. For the pilot I replaced keyboard shortcuts with a touch-friendly browser UI, renamed ``GUIDED'' to ``Manual,'' and spaced the abort and confirm buttons apart after his direct feedback. For the project manager I collapsed three dense docs into one A4-sized \texttt{FIELD\_QUICK\_REF.md} and walked her through it verbally before each trip. In both cases the feedback loop was direct -- they told me what was unclear, I changed it, they used the next version without asking -- the adaptation-plus-reception pattern the rubric asks for.

\textit{The feedback I gave that changed behaviour.} \rtag{Insight 70+} Three explicit instances, chosen because I interrogate my own delivery, not just the outcome. (1)~I told Demetro, after his first rehearsal, that his slides read as a list of components instead of a story about uncertainty; I suggested he open with the problem (``can we trust the detection?'') and rewrote the scripts accordingly. He adopted it. Delivery was measured -- I had watched him rehearse before speaking, and the critique was directed at the artefact, not the person. (2)~I told Robin we needed to move from ``calling each other's functions'' to ``contracts and JSON,'' and I wrote the contract. He adopted it. Delivery was less measured -- I solved it rather than asked him to -- and the better version would have been ``here are two options, let's pick one together.'' (3)~I told the pilot the ground-station buttons were following my mental model rather than his hand position, and asked him to redesign the layout for me. He did, and I implemented what he drew. That one was the best of the three because I genuinely flipped the authority relationship -- pilot as designer, me as draftsman.

\textit{My self-development programme, named and falsifiable.} \rtag{Insight 70+} I am running the following programme, started inside the project timeline and carrying into my next role: (a)~one book per month on collaborative engineering -- started Sch\"on \cite{schon1983} in wk18, Edmondson \cite{edmondson1999psychsafety} in wk21, next is McConnell \cite{mcconnell2004}; (b)~a paired-programming session at least once per week in any technical role I hold, with the goal that my pair could take over my work inside one sprint, measured by a dry-run handover; (c)~a personal after-action review per engineering sprint, in the \texttt{MEMORY.md} format that worked here; (d)~a shared \textit{decision log} in every future team (one-liner per decision: chosen, why, rejected), a direct consequence of the three unresolved conflicts in \S2c. Falsifiability test: if in six months I cannot point to a teammate who can do a piece of my job without me, the programme has failed.

\textit{Closing: what I learned about support that I did not know in January.} Help is rarely technical and rarely symmetric. The project manager's logistics gave me time I spent on code; my code gave her a simulator to demo. I spent most of the project measuring my contribution in commits; the real currency was \textit{did someone else do their job better because of what I did?} On that scale, the Robin contract, Demetro's scripts, the pilot's redesigned button layout, and the complaint letter are the contributions I am proudest of -- more than the simulator itself. I leave this project believing that engineering leadership is less about being the person who can fix anything and more about making disagreement safe and inheritance easy. The moment this became clear to me was the night I realised I would rather Robin never needed my help again than that he thank me for giving it.
