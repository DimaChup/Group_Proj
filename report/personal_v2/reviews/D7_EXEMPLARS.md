# D7 EXEMPLARS — What Distinction-Level (70+) Reflective Reports Look Like

> Reference guide for calibrating the D7 rewrite. Compiled from Hatton & Smith, Moon, Gibbs, Kolb, Schön, the Edinburgh REFLECT rubric, Sussex final-year project criteria, Bristol AQPO marking framework, and engineering-specific reflective writing literature. Use this as a checklist — every paragraph the rewriter produces should pass these tests.

---

## 1. The core distinction: depth, not detail

The single biggest differentiator between Merit (60-69) and Distinction (70+) is NOT how much happened on the project — it is how deeply the writer interrogates what happened. Hatton & Smith's four-level model is the canonical framework:

| Level | Name | What it looks like | Typical mark band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Descriptive writing | "We built X. I did Y. The team met on Tuesdays." | Fail / low Pass |
| 2 | Descriptive reflection | "We built X. I did Y because I thought it would help." (reasons given, but unexamined) | Pass / low Merit |
| 3 | Dialogic reflection | "We built X. I did Y because I thought it would help — but in hindsight the team needed Z, and I now see I chose Y partly because it was familiar." (multiple perspectives, internal dialogue, self-questioning) | Merit / low Distinction |
| 4 | Critical reflection | Level 3 + explicit examination of assumptions, frames of reference, power dynamics, what-I-would-do-differently-and-why, and connection to theory or wider context. | Distinction (70+) |

**Key insight:** Level 3 hits ~68. Level 4 hits 72+. The rewriter must push every reflective paragraph from Level 3 to Level 4 by adding the "and I now see this is because..." move.

---

## 2. Characteristic verbs and phrases at each level

### Distinction (70+) vocabulary — USE THESE
- "In hindsight, my assumption that X was driven by..."
- "What I initially framed as a technical problem was actually a communication problem because..."
- "I now recognise that my bias toward [behaviour] cost the team [specific outcome]..."
- "Had I challenged [decision] earlier, we would have saved [measurable thing]..."
- "This experience forced me to reframe my belief that..."
- "The uncomfortable realisation was that..."
- "On reflection, the root cause was not [surface reason] but [deeper cause]..."
- "I was wrong about..."
- "The tension between [value A] and [value B] is something I had not previously noticed in myself..."
- "If I had to do this again, I would [specific action], because [reasoning grounded in what happened]..."

### Merit (60-69) vocabulary — AVOID OR UPGRADE
- "I learned a lot about teamwork." (vague)
- "It was challenging but rewarding." (cliché, no analysis)
- "Communication is important." (platitude, no specific incident)
- "I will try to communicate better in the future." (no mechanism, no evidence)
- "The team worked well together." (no critical eye)
- "I enjoyed the project." (feelings without analysis)
- "We overcame the problem." (no named problem, no named mechanism)

### Pass-level vocabulary — NEVER USE
- "First we did X. Then we did Y. Then we did Z." (pure chronology)
- "Everyone contributed their best." (empty praise)
- "I think I did my best." (no evidence, no self-criticism)

## 3. What kinds of incidents Distinction writers cite

Top-mark reflective writing is built on **specific, named, concrete incidents** — not general impressions of "the project." Exemplar incidents tend to be:

1. **A moment you were wrong.** The writer held a technical or interpersonal position, turned out to be mistaken, names the mistake, and dissects why they held it.
2. **A decision point with a counterfactual.** "We chose A over B. Here is what actually happened, and here is what would have happened if we had chosen B — and I can now say with evidence which was right."
3. **A moment of friction that wasn't resolved cleanly.** Distinction writers do NOT pretend conflicts were resolved by "better communication." They describe the actual mechanism (who said what to whom, what changed).
4. **A piece of feedback you gave or received that stung.** The writer examines both their emotional reaction AND what they now think was the correct response.
5. **A leadership moment where you had no formal authority.** (See Section 5.)
6. **A time your own behaviour contradicted your stated values.** Extremely high marks — rare, brave, and demonstrates metacognition.
7. **A specific number that tells a story.** "We lost 3 days rebuilding the test rig because I had not verified the connector pinout — an hour of due diligence would have saved 24 hours of team time."

**Rule for the rewriter:** every major reflective claim must anchor to ONE concrete incident. No floating generalities.

---

## 4. The type of self-criticism that is rewarded

Distinction reports self-criticise in a very specific way. It must be:

- **Specific:** name the behaviour, not the trait. ("I dominated the week-3 architecture meeting by interrupting Alex twice" — NOT "I can be impatient".)
- **Actionable:** pair the criticism with a concrete mechanism for doing it differently, tested against actual evidence from the project.
- **Proportionate:** don't flagellate over trivia, don't minimise real mistakes.
- **Forward-looking:** show that the lesson has already changed behaviour within the project, not just in some hypothetical future.
- **Structural, not dispositional:** the best self-criticism identifies a pattern or a system problem, not a personality flaw. ("I defaulted to solving problems alone when stressed, which prevented the team from seeing early warning signs" is a pattern. "I am introverted" is a trait.)

**FAILURE MODE to avoid:** *defensive self-criticism*. Phrases like "in hindsight I could have done X but given the constraints this was unavoidable" — this is self-criticism that retracts itself. Markers spot it instantly and it caps the paragraph at Merit.

**FAILURE MODE to avoid:** *vague self-flattery disguised as criticism*. "My main weakness was caring too much about quality." This is a humblebrag and kills credibility.

## 5. How leadership evidence is evaluated at Distinction level

Distinction markers do NOT reward formal titles ("I was the team lead") — they reward **observable influence on outcomes**. The hierarchy:

1. **Lowest value:** "I was appointed lead." (role, not action)
2. **Pass value:** "As lead, I organised meetings and set deadlines." (administrative)
3. **Merit value:** "I proposed X and the team adopted it." (one-off contribution)
4. **Distinction value:** "When the team was deadlocked on X, I ran a structured comparison of options A/B/C on a shared doc, invited Alex (who had been quiet) to lead the analysis of B, and we converged on a decision within 48 hours. I chose this approach because two weeks earlier I had watched a similar deadlock consume 5 days, and I now believe the failure mode was an absence of structured option comparison, not a lack of technical ideas."
5. **High Distinction:** Level 4 + you led WITHOUT formal authority, and you name the specific influence mechanism (facilitation, question-asking, modelling behaviour, offering to absorb risk, connecting two people, etc.).

**The rewriter's test:** for every leadership claim, ask "would a marker believe this changed the trajectory of the project, and can I point to the evidence?"

## 6. How top writers handle conflict

Rank-ordered from weakest to strongest:

1. **Avoid** — don't mention any conflict. (Kills you at Merit ceiling; markers assume either no insight or dishonesty.)
2. **Describe** — "There was a disagreement about X." (Pass.)
3. **Analyse** — "The disagreement about X was a task conflict masquerading as a relationship conflict because Alex and I had different priors about risk tolerance." (Merit.)
4. **Show learning** — Level 3 + "I now see I escalated it by [specific behaviour], and the next time a similar tension arose in week 8 I [different specific behaviour], which [measurable outcome]." (Distinction.)
5. **Show structural insight** — Level 4 + "More broadly, this taught me that teams of high-agency individuals default to parallel solo work under stress, which hides disagreements until they become expensive. Systems for making disagreement visible early (like decision logs) are not bureaucracy — they are insurance." (High Distinction.)

**Critical rule:** never claim a conflict was resolved by "better communication." That phrase is a tell for surface reflection. Name the actual mechanism.

## 7. Feedback given to teammates — the Insight 72+ gate

At most UK engineering Master's programmes, the **Insight** criterion caps at ~68 unless the writer demonstrates they gave usable feedback to teammates AND examined their own behaviour as a feedback-giver. The pattern that scores:

> "In week 6 I told [teammate] that their [specific work product] was [specific concrete problem] and suggested [specific alternative]. I did this because [reasoning]. Their reaction was [honest description]. Looking back, I delivered the message [well/badly] because [analysis]. What I would change is [specific mechanism]. What I learned about giving feedback to high-performing peers is [generalisable insight]."

Key features:
- Feedback given is named and specific (not "I gave feedback").
- The reception is described honestly (not "they took it well").
- The writer interrogates **their own** delivery, not the recipient's response.
- A generalisable lesson is extracted and (ideally) shown being applied later in the project.

**Failure mode:** "I gave constructive feedback when needed." This is the single most common Merit-ceiling sentence and should never appear.

## 8. Distinction-level opening paragraphs — what they do

Merit openers recap the project. Distinction openers do ONE of these:

1. **Drop into a specific scene** that will be the anchor for the whole reflection. "At 23:47 on the night before the flight demo, I was in the lab deleting my own code."
2. **State the thesis of the reflection.** "This project taught me that my reflex to solve problems alone is not a strength — it is a failure mode that only looked like a strength in smaller-scale work."
3. **Name the uncomfortable truth upfront.** "I will argue in this report that the thing I am most proud of in this project — the autonomous state machine — is also the thing that most exposed a weakness I had not previously been willing to see."

All three create immediate signal of Level 4 reflection. All three avoid the dead phrase "This report will reflect on my experience in the SAR drone project."

---

## 9. Distinction-level closing paragraphs — what they do

Merit closings summarise what was learned. Distinction closings do ALL of these:

1. **Name what will actually change in future behaviour**, with a mechanism, not a wish. ("I will keep a decision log in every future team project" — not "I will communicate better").
2. **Acknowledge what is still unresolved** — the problem you have identified but not yet solved. (Shows intellectual honesty; markers love this.)
3. **Connect the personal lesson to the professional identity.** "I entered this project thinking engineering leadership was technical excellence plus organisation. I leave it thinking it is mostly the skill of making disagreement safe."
4. **Avoid triumph.** Distinction closings rarely end with "and it was a success." They end with "and here is the lesson I will carry."

---

## 10. Metrics vs narrative prose

Top-mark reflective writing **uses numbers sparingly but precisely** as anchors for narrative claims. The rule:

- **Good:** "Three days lost" / "I gave feedback in week 6 and again in week 9" / "My first estimate was 40 hours; the actual was 110" — numbers that make a claim falsifiable.
- **Bad:** tables of metrics, progress bars, or "I spent 32% of my time on CV and 28% on planning" — this is project-report padding, not reflection.

Tables are uncommon in Distinction-level reflective reports and their presence often signals the writer is hiding from reflection behind data. One small table (e.g., before/after of a behaviour) is acceptable; more than one is usually a warning sign.

---

## 11. Page density and formatting conventions (UK Master's reflective report, 10pt body, 2cm margins)

- At 10pt single-spaced with 2cm margins on A4, expect roughly **600-750 words per full page** of prose.
- A typical 5-page D7 reflective report lands at **3000-3700 words**.
- Distinction-level reports are **dense** — very little white space, no decorative headings, no bulleted lists longer than 4 items, no tables of contents for a 5-page document.
- Short paragraphs (3-5 sentences) are preferred over long ones; each paragraph should advance one reflective move.
- Headings are functional (e.g., "Teamwork", "Self-management", "What I would change"), not creative.

## 12. References and bibliography

- **Expected but not essential.** A D7-style reflective report scoring in the 70s usually contains **2-6 references**, placed exactly where a reflective claim connects to a named model (Gibbs, Kolb, Schön, Hatton & Smith, Moon, Tuckman, Belbin).
- References are used to *anchor* reflection, not to decorate it. A single well-used Schön citation ("knowing-in-action vs reflection-in-action") is worth more than ten generic citations.
- Bibliography is short (half a page maximum). Harvard or IEEE, consistent with the rest of the submission.
- **Failure mode:** citing Gibbs or Kolb in the introduction, then never using the model to structure any actual reflection. Markers treat this as decoration and it does not lift the mark.

## 13. COMMON FAILURES AT MERIT LEVEL (60-69) THAT KEEP REPORTS OUT OF DISTINCTION

The rewriter MUST scan for and eliminate every one of these patterns:

1. **The chronological trap.** Structuring the report week-by-week or phase-by-phase. This forces description and starves reflection. Structure by *theme* or *lesson*, not by calendar.
2. **Generalised teamwork platitudes.** "I learned that teamwork is important." "Communication is key." These are automatic Merit-ceilings.
3. **Hidden self-praise.** "My main weakness was that I cared too much about quality." Markers spot this in one second and discount the whole report.
4. **Unsupported claims of leadership.** "I took a leadership role." Where? When? What changed because of it?
5. **Conflict-free narratives.** No disagreements named, no friction described. Markers assume either dishonesty or lack of insight.
6. **Feedback-giving described in passive voice.** "Feedback was provided." (Who? To whom? About what? How did they react?)
7. **The defensive retract.** "I could have done X but the time constraints made it impossible." This erases the reflection.
8. **Triumphalism.** Ending with "the project was a huge success and I am proud of what we achieved." Distinction closings are sober.
9. **Tools-and-technologies padding.** "I learned Python, OpenCV, and MAVLink." This is a CV bullet, not reflection.
10. **Citing Gibbs without using him.** Decorative citations with no structural consequence.
11. **Vague future commitments.** "In future I will communicate better / plan better / be more assertive." No mechanism = no credit.
12. **Hiding behind the team.** "We decided..." "We realised..." The reflection must be in first-person singular. "I" must appear in every reflective paragraph.
13. **Metrics as a substitute for reflection.** A progress chart is not a reflection. A table of contributions is not a reflection.
14. **Perfect-past syndrome.** Describing what happened as if it was always obvious in the moment. Distinction writers are honest about what they didn't see until later.
15. **Feedback-free feedback paragraphs.** Claiming to have given feedback without naming what was said or how it was received.
16. **Equating "learning about X" with "reflecting on X".** Learning is what went in. Reflection is what changed.
17. **Over-reliance on Gibbs' six stages as a rigid template.** Using Gibbs as a scaffold is fine; reproducing the six headings in order screams undergraduate.
18. **Bulleted lists where prose is needed.** Reflection lives in sentences. Lists kill nuance.
19. **Closing with a "lessons learned" bullet list.** This is a project-management artefact, not a reflection. Prose only in the conclusion.
20. **Starting with "This report will..."** The most common Merit-ceiling opener in UK engineering reflection. Ban it.

## 14. TEN CONCRETE PHRASE / PATTERN RECOMMENDATIONS FOR THE REWRITER

Each of these is a move that lifts a paragraph from Merit to Distinction. Use them literally, or as templates.

1. **"The uncomfortable realisation was that..."** — forces the writer into Level 4 territory by naming a belief-revision moment.

2. **"I initially framed this as [X] — in fact it was [Y], and I now see I preferred the [X] framing because it protected me from [Z]."** — the reframing-plus-motive move, characteristic of critical reflection.

3. **"I told [Alex] in week [N] that [specific thing]. Their reaction was [honest]. My delivery was [self-critical analysis]. What I would do differently is [concrete mechanism]."** — the feedback-giving template that unlocks the Insight 72+ band.

4. **"Had I acted on [X] a week earlier, we would have saved [specific measurable outcome]. The reason I did not was [honest dissection of own reasoning], which is a pattern I now recognise in myself."** — the counterfactual-plus-pattern move.

5. **"This experience forced me to reframe my belief that [previous belief]. I now think [new belief] — and the evidence that convinced me was [specific incident]."** — explicit belief-revision, heavily rewarded.

6. **"I was wrong about [specific claim]. [Teammate] was right. I dismissed their view in [specific moment] because [honest reason]. The lesson is not that I should always defer — it is that I should [specific mechanism]."** — the admitting-error move, done without collapsing into generic humility.

7. **"What looked like a technical problem was actually a [communication / trust / incentive] problem, because [mechanism]."** — the surface-to-root-cause move, a signature of Distinction-level analysis.

8. **"The tension between my commitment to [value A] and my commitment to [value B] surfaced in [specific incident]. I had not previously noticed that these two values could conflict in me."** — the self-discovery move, very rare and very highly rewarded.

9. **"I will [specific behavioural change] in future team projects, and the way I will know whether it is working is [specific observable signal]."** — forward-looking commitment with a falsifiability test, not a vague aspiration.

10. **"I leave this project believing that engineering leadership is less about [what I used to think] and more about [what I now think]. The moment this became clear to me was [specific incident]."** — the identity-shift closing, a hallmark of high-Distinction conclusions.

---

## 15. Quick scoring heuristic for the rewriter

For every paragraph the rewriter produces, check:

- [ ] Is there a specific named incident? (If no → rewrite)
- [ ] Is "I" the subject, not "we"? (If no → rewrite)
- [ ] Does it revise a belief, name an error, or reframe a prior view? (If no → Merit ceiling)
- [ ] Is the self-criticism specific, actionable, proportionate? (If no → rewrite)
- [ ] Does it avoid all 20 Merit failure modes from Section 13? (If no → rewrite)
- [ ] Is the forward-looking commitment falsifiable? (If no → add mechanism)
- [ ] Would a cynical marker be able to say "but did this actually change anything?" (If yes → add evidence)

Target: every page of the rewritten D7 should contain at least TWO Level-4 moves from Section 14, and zero of the failure modes from Section 13.
