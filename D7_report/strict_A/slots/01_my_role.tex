\section{My Role}

\textbf{1a.}
After our first team meeting we divided the work among five: myself, Robin, Harish, Edward, and Zian. Harish took hardware setup and inter-component communications. Zian owned path planning. Robin owned the state machine. Edward and I shared computer vision---I delegated CV model training to Edward and kept the detection pipeline, calibration, and integration. That was the explicit split, and on paper it looked clean.

In practice my role drifted far beyond CV. I built the full detection-to-GPS-coordinate pipeline---a much bigger job than anyone anticipated. A single camera detection doesn't give you a GPS position; you need focal length calibration, altitude compensation, and pitch-and-roll correction because the drone's attitude shifts the image centre away from the nadir point. Individual GPS estimates had significant scatter, so I developed and benchmarked multiple averaging techniques---inverse-variance weighting, spatial clustering, Kalman filtering---each with different trade-offs. I tested all of these against the one real flight video we had (provided by Sid), which was literally all our real data. I used it for model evaluation, for retraining, and for deducing ground truth to benchmark my averaging methods against.

Alongside CV I took on the simulation framework as a ``side thing.'' It didn't stay small. With hardware that wasn't working, my laptop was the only place where forward progress was possible. The simulation grew into a thorough model of the full mission---GPS drift, camera shake, detection throttling, NFZ geofencing---and I ran it thousands of times, thinking through failure modes. I also built dashboards, monitoring tools, progressive test scripts for step-by-step confidence building, post-processing scripts for DJI footage, and field calibration tools. None of this was in my original brief. It happened because I kept seeing gaps between where the team was and where a working demo needed to be, and I couldn't leave them unfilled. In hindsight, Tuckman (1965) would say we jumped from Forming into task execution without building the interpersonal trust that makes later conflict productive---our Forming was one meeting. When I look at the drift in my role, Lencioni's (2002) framework explains part of it: building infrastructure alone was a way of feeling productive without having to slow down and bring others along. My instinct under pressure is that one person delivers. This project taught me that instinct is a failure mode dressed as a strength.

\textbf{1b.}
I'm most proud of the simulation work and the CV-to-GPS estimation pipeline. Given extremely limited real data---one flight video from Sid---I managed to build a realistic way to estimate a casualty's GPS position from camera detections, calibrate the camera properly in the lab (field of view, focal length at measured distances), and create an end-to-end simulation that could verify all twelve project requirements. On the first cancelled flight day, instead of going home, I calibrated the focal length, ran lens calibration, built field reference documents, and closed a stack of commits before dark. That day crystallised something: a missing drone must not become a missing report. The simulation framework is the only reason this project produced verifiable engineering output at all.

Edward trained a CV model as delegated, but we didn't end up using it---we went with a standard YOLO person detection model, playing it safe. The reasons were sound: not enough flight days to collect realistic aerial training data, and we couldn't validate a custom model in real conditions. \textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: did you discuss this decision with Edward directly? \textbf{1c.}
What I'd focus on differently is the gap between \emph{capability} and \emph{enablement}. I spent 3--4$\times$ more hours than expected on this project. Much of that time went into tools and infrastructure that only I could operate. In future work I want to spend less time building alone and more time making sure teammates can use, modify, and extend what I build without needing to ask me. The falsifiable signal I'll watch for: can a teammate complete a task using my code within 48~hours of receiving it without coming back with clarifying questions? If they can't, my documentation has failed, not their attention.
