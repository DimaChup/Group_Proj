\section{My Impact}

\textbf{3a.}
The CV pipeline and the simulation framework were my two largest contributions to the team's output. I'll explain the mechanism behind each, because ``I built a lot'' isn't a useful claim---what matters is how the work drove the project forward and where it fell short.

The CV pipeline started with my formal responsibility: detection. But detection alone is nearly useless to a SAR drone. You need to convert a pixel bounding box into a GPS coordinate the flight controller can act on. That conversion is deceptively expensive. It requires calibrated focal length, real-time altitude, and attitude correction for pitch and roll---because the drone is almost never perfectly level, and even a few degrees of tilt shift the image centre away from the nadir point. I did thorough camera calibration in the lab: field of view at measured distances, focal length measurements, lens distortion mapping via checkerboard. Single detection estimates had significant scatter, so I developed and tested multiple averaging approaches---inverse-variance weighting, spatial clustering, Kalman filtering---each with different trade-offs in latency versus accuracy. I benchmarked all of them against ground truth I deduced from the one real flight video Sid provided. That single video was literally all our real aerial data, and I squeezed everything I could out of it: model evaluation, retraining data, calibration validation, and GPS accuracy benchmarking.

The pipeline's interface was deliberately clean. Robin's state machine could call the detection function and get back a found/not-found flag, pixel coordinates, and a confidence score without knowing anything about the internals. When Robin needed GPS estimates for his flight logic, the interface extended naturally. That separation meant we could develop in parallel---he didn't wait for me, and I didn't break his code when I changed averaging methods or swapped models.

Edward trained a CV model as I'd delegated. We didn't end up using it. We went with a standard YOLO person detection model instead, and the reasoning was sound: we didn't have enough flight days to collect realistic aerial training data, and we couldn't run proper experiments to validate a custom model in real outdoor conditions. We did have trained models for detecting specific items of interest, but the YOLO person detector was the safer choice given our limited real-world testing. The simulation came out of frustration turned productive. After the first two practice days produced nothing---faulty hardware, no GPS to test against, the whole team demoralised---I had a choice: wait for the next flight window or find a way to make progress without a drone in the air. I chose simulation. What started as a quick sanity check grew into a thorough model of the full mission: GPS drift, camera shake, detection throttling at realistic inference speeds, the complete state machine, and all twelve project requirements verifiable end-to-end. I ran it thousands of times, watching closely, thinking through what could go wrong. Instead of holding failure modes in my head, I had something concrete to test against. Robin suggested the repulsive force concept for NFZ avoidance; I thought it was a great idea, got into it, and spent several days experimenting with edge cases---what happens when the target sits right on the no-fly zone boundary, how the repulsive field should decay with distance, what override behaviour the pilot needs. The geofence and safety layer that came out of this was one of the more satisfying pieces of engineering in the project.

I also built dashboards and monitoring tools---a passive watch dashboard for detection during manual flight, a diagnostics dashboard showing all drone subsystems at once with live model switching---all running headlessly on the Pi through a browser. I wrote progressive test scripts forming a safety-critical test ladder: hardware checks, passive observation, waypoint tests, then full autonomous runs, each designed to build confidence before adding risk. The risk assessment framework from Graham's Professional Practice lectures (the five-step HSE process) shaped how I structured that progression: identify hazards, determine who might be harmed, evaluate risks, record findings, review.

Everything came together on the actual flight day without prior real-hardware testing of the full integrated pipeline. That worked because the simulation had been thorough---but it was also somewhat lucky. In risk management terms, we accepted a higher residual risk than I was comfortable with, because we'd simply run out of time. The fact that the demo went well says something about the quality of the preparatory work, but I'd have been much more confident if we'd had time to run the progressive test ladder step by step.

\textbf{3b.}
The honest gap is that I built things only I could maintain. Nobody else on the team edited the simulation code or the CV pipeline. If I'd been unavailable, the team's most complex modules would have been unreadable to anyone else. I built things that worked, but I didn't build things others could extend. That's a prototype, not engineering.

In future projects I'd refocus on three things. First, every tool I build needs at least one named teammate who can use it independently---if I can't identify that person before I start coding, the design needs rethinking. Second, I'd pair on code reviews for any file over 500 lines rather than refactoring solo. Third, I'd invest earlier in documentation that isn't just technically complete but genuinely usable by someone who didn't write the code. The test: can a teammate modify the module within 48 hours without asking me how? If they can't, my documentation has failed, not their attention.

I'd also spend less time retreating into technical work when things got frustrating. When hardware failed and the team was demoralised, my instinct was to go heads-down on my laptop and build things. That was productive in output terms, but it wasn't leadership. Graham's observation that ``the forced detachment from the technical work which might have been the reason why you entered engineering can be too much to bear'' hit close to home.
