\section{My team}

\textbf{2a.} At the wk12 kickoff we did hold an explicit structure conversation, and I want to credit Robin for forcing it---I would've skipped it. We split on module ownership: Robin took flight execution, I took perception and simulation, Edward handled the Cube and hardware interface, Demetro took client-facing communication and the FDR presentation, and our PM coordinated schedule and budget. We deliberately declined to name a single technical lead because the brief devolves autonomy-level choices to the team and we wanted that authority distributed. Tuckman (1965) calls this Forming, and Graham's Professional Practice lecture warned us that ``too little time spent forming, which means no building of trust, means jumping straight into the storming phase.'' That's exactly what happened. Our Forming lasted one meeting.

The structure then drifted twice. The first drift was silent: around wk16, as hardware kept slipping, the project's centre of gravity migrated from the bench to my laptop---the only surface where forward progress was possible. Nobody declared it; it just happened. In Lencioni's (2002) terms, this was a \emph{lack of commitment} born from ambiguity: we'd divided tasks but hadn't committed to a cadence for re-evaluating that division when the environment changed. The second drift was loud. At wk17, after I caught Robin and myself building overlapping state machines in parallel, I asked for a renegotiation. We agreed in writing (\texttt{docs/DESIGN\_DECISIONS.md:107--109}) that his script would own the flight loop and call my \texttt{passive\_watch} as an HTTP service for detections. I now see we'd optimised for task division over communication cadence because task division felt unambiguous and cadence felt like overhead---and the first silent drift happened precisely because we had no routine for restating structure when the environment shifted. Unexplicit structure is a luxury that only survives in slack-rich teams, and ours had no slack.

\textbf{2b.} The most impactful team-working element, measured by what it unblocked, was a ratio I hadn't previously noticed: the four hours I spent writing the \texttt{VISION\_PIPELINE\_FOR\_COLLEAGUE.md} handoff doc and the \texttt{--robin} integration flag (commit \texttt{a5b1ab7}) bought Robin roughly twenty hours of unblocked integration time across wk20. He wired \texttt{passive\_watch} into his mission script without asking me a single clarifying question for two days. I now understand those docs worked because they traded my solo time for Robin's onboarding time at roughly 5:1---better team economics than I'd assumed documentation ever delivered. I had previously treated handoff docs as a cost I paid for politeness, not as a multiplier.

The conflict I need to name happened in wk17. I'd proposed an 11-state FSM for \texttt{main.py} with explicit transitions, timeout guards, and a geofence repulsor. Robin and Demetro pushed back in the same Teams thread---Robin arguing that a two-out-of-five maintainability floor meant the FSM was over-engineered, Demetro arguing the extra states wouldn't survive a hardware-debug session with Edward. My first reaction was defensive. I genuinely believed the FSM was safer given our NFZ edge cases, and commit \texttt{ce5c036} later proved part of that belief correct when the RTL mode tuple fix needed an explicit state transition to \texttt{LANDING}. But I took 24 hours, reread Robin's flight script, and realised half my insistence was that the FSM was \emph{my} code. Lencioni (2002) would call this the base of the pyramid: I was ring-fencing my work because I didn't trust the team to navigate it, and that ring-fencing looked like technical conviction when it was actually territorial reflex. I proposed a compromise on the wk17 call: I'd keep the full FSM for the autonomous path in \texttt{main.py} but strip it to a lightweight polling structure exposed over HTTP for Robin's script. Robin accepted, Demetro signed off, and the wk20 integration took an afternoon instead of a week. I was right about the FSM and wrong about the context---and ``is this the right design'' and ``is this the right design \emph{for this team}'' are two kinds of rightness that can come apart.

A second, quieter conflict surfaced in wk20 over workload distribution after three cancelled flight days. Instead of confronting the PM directly---something I historically do badly---I drafted a complaint letter in measured factual prose, iterated the tone six times across five commits, and shared it only after Robin cut three sentences that were ``technically accurate but personally sharp.'' The PM disagreed with one paragraph; we rewrote it together and the letter went out with all five names. The mediating action was \emph{externalising an internal conflict}: I'd not previously noticed that I can only handle confrontation when it's mediated by writing. That's a pattern across my personal life, not just this project, and I don't yet know whether it's a strength I should lean into or a limitation I should challenge.

\textbf{2c.} Three concrete changes, each with a falsifiable signal. First, I'd institute a weekly one-line decision log from wk1---every call that bound more than one person's work, with date, owner, and reason. The silent wk16 drift would've been caught by any written record; signal: zero overlapping-work surprises at integration checkpoints. Second, I'd front-load interface contracts \emph{before} parallel work begins---a two-hour whiteboard session in the first week of any new module to agree inputs, outputs, and failure modes at every seam. A single wk14 session on the \texttt{passive\_watch} HTTP contract would have killed the whole wk17 conflict. Third, I'd stop treating documentation as a substitute for conversation. The two pieces of writing that actually changed teammates' behaviour were the 400-word pivot message and the complaint letter---both short, timely, paired with a live call. The 3000-word colleague doc sat unread until Robin finally needed it. Signal for next time: any document longer than 500 words must be paired with a 15-minute walkthrough inside a week, or I'll treat it as unsent.
