\section{My Support}

\textbf{4a.}
Robin was the teammate who helped me work most effectively, and I can point to the specific mechanism. After the Easter hardware fix, Robin needed my detection output for his state machine. That meant we had to agree on an interface: what data my pipeline would provide, in what format, at what rate. Working through that contract forced me to make my code cleaner and my GPS estimation more robust than it would have been if I'd only been building for myself. Robin was proactive---he pushed back when things weren't clear and held me to delivering something he could actually use on a specific day. That pressure made my work better. In Lencioni's (2002) terms, we'd built enough trust at the base of the pyramid that disagreement was productive rather than political: I could say ``this estimate is noisy, give me a day'' and he'd say ``fine, but I need it by Thursday,'' and that was a conversation, not a conflict.

Robin also suggested the repulsive force concept for NFZ avoidance. I could have treated that as an intrusion into my module, but I thought it was a genuinely good idea and ran with it. The fact that we could exchange ideas as equals---rather than defending territorial boundaries---was what made the integration period work.

\textbf{4b.}
What I did for the team splits into technical enablement and communication, and I want to be honest about which actually landed and which didn't.

The technical contributions that \emph{did} land: the CV pipeline interface let Robin's state machine consume detection output without touching my code. Once the interface existed, Robin could develop his flight logic in parallel. I also built the simulation as something the whole team could reference---I communicated the work to the team, and they were happy with it. Robin used it as a reference point for his integration work. The safety-critical layer---zone repulsive fields, geofencing, kill switch handling---was designed so that any team member operating the system would be protected from dangerous edge cases without needing to understand the internals.

The technical contributions that \emph{didn't} land as intended: the progressive test scripts. I designed a full safety-critical test ladder---bench checks through passive flight observation to full autonomous mission---each step building confidence before adding risk. Due to limited flight time, the suite was never fully executed on real hardware. The scripts were well-designed in isolation, but I didn't tie them to a specific team activity or flight day plan tightly enough for them to get used. In Lencioni's framework, accountability only works when commitments are specific---vague intentions to ``make tools available'' don't drive behaviour the way ``we're running test 2 at 10am tomorrow'' does. That's a lesson I'll carry forward: tooling without a named user and a deadline is tooling that gathers dust.

The broader pattern I see is that my strongest support contributions were the ones with a named recipient. The CV interface worked because Robin needed it on a specific day. The simulation was useful because it solved a concrete problem the whole team could see. The test scripts and dashboards were technically sound but lacked that same pull---nobody was waiting for them. In future I'd make sure every piece of infrastructure I build has someone who's asked for it, not just someone who might use it.
