Running Context — Pure Text (auto-updated from Apollo's dumps)

This is the ongoing merged narrative of everything Apollo has shared. No duplicates. Keeps growing as more context is provided.

There were 5 of us: Apollo, Robin, Harish, Edward, and Zian.

After the first team meeting we looked at all the tasks that might be needed and split them up. Harish was responsible for hardware setup and communications between components. Zian was responsible for path planning. Robin was responsible for state machines. Apollo and Edward shared responsibility for computer vision — Apollo delegated the CV model training to Edward.

Edward and Zian were not very proactive. They would do what they were told to do, and it seemed like they did a lot of work, but they weren't self-directed. Edward did train a CV model as delegated, but we didn't end up using it — went with a standard YOLO model instead. The reasons: not enough flight days to collect realistic aerial training data, couldn't run proper experiments to validate a custom model.

Apollo's formal responsibility was computer vision, but in practice he took on far more. He built the full CV pipeline: detection to GPS coordinate conversion, using calibration and altitude compensation. This required pitch and roll correction because the drone's attitude affects coordinate accuracy. Single GPS estimates from detections had significant error, so he developed and tested multiple averaging techniques. He tested all of these against the one real flight video provided by Sid — that single video was all the real data available, so he made the most of it.

Apollo did thorough camera calibration in the lab — field of view, focal length measurements. He created post-processing scripts for the DJI footage from Sid, calibration scripts for camera parameters, and video analysis tools — all designed to maximise learning from extremely limited real flight data.

He built multiple dashboards and monitoring tools: a passive watch dashboard for monitoring and detection during manual flight, a diagnostics dashboard showing all drone subsystems at once, with the ability to switch between different vision models live to compare performance. All built for the Pi (headless, browser-based) so they could run on actual drone hardware.

He wrote progressive test scripts for empirical drone performance testing — blur effect at different speeds, detection range vs altitude, detection rate at various conditions. These formed a safety-critical test ladder designed for step-by-step confidence building before attempting a full mission. Due to limited flight time, the full test suite was never run on real hardware.

In parallel, Apollo built an in-depth simulation of the entire mission. This was because the team had limited flight days and the hardware wasn't working. He wanted the team to keep moving forward despite hardware holdups, and the simulation served as both a backup and a way to anticipate issues. He built a safety-critical layer with zone repulsive fields and geofencing. Robin suggested the repulsive force concept for NFZ avoidance — Apollo developed and implemented it over several days of experimentation, testing edge cases like what happens when the dummy is placed very close to the no-fly zone boundary. The simulation was thoroughly tested and could verify all R01-R12 requirements. Apollo spent 3-4x more hours than expected on this project, largely because of the simulation work.

The team did a lot of preparatory work, but when it came to flight days they got quite disheartened and demotivated because they didn't know what actually worked and what didn't until they actually flew the drone. Many things could only be tested with real GPS signal, which wasn't available in the lab. So the team could only test GPS-dependent features on flight days — and flight time was extremely limited.

There were only two practice flight days and then a third day which was the performance for guests. On the first two practice days the team had everything prepared, but they were given faulty hardware. Things just didn't work, and they weren't sure what the issues were. This was very frustrating.

There were also organisational problems on flight days. The Wi-Fi signal was prioritised for teams that were actively flying their drones, while the other teams were supposed to be tinkering with their drones on the ground. But without good Wi-Fi, the team couldn't connect to their drone to test or configure things. IP address and naming conflicts added to the frustration. The hardware that wasn't working wasn't the team's responsibility — it was provided to them. The organisation of flight day logistics could have been better, though this should be mentioned diplomatically since the course organiser (who marks the report) was in charge of organising it.

Luckily, before the third day (demo day), there was an Easter holiday period that included two extra flight days. During these, things started working — the hardware issue was resolved. So on the actual demo day the team managed to demonstrate something functional, even though they had planned for something much more advanced.

After sorting out hardware issues, Robin needed detection output for his state machine. Apollo provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. Building this interface was "quite expensive" — it required attitude correction for pitch and roll, altitude compensation, and dealing with significant single-estimate error. Ideas were discussed between Apollo and Robin as colleagues. Robin's state machine consumed Apollo's CV output.

Everything came together on the actual flight day without prior real-hardware testing of the full pipeline. This worked because the simulation was thorough — but it was also somewhat lucky. Apollo would have preferred to run the full progressive test ladder step-by-step to build confidence that the real mission could be done safely. The test infrastructure existed for exactly this purpose but was never fully executed due to time constraints.
