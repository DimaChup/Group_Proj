\section{My team}

\textbf{2a.}~At the wk12 kickoff we split on module ownership: Robin took flight execution, I took perception, Harish took hardware and comms, Edward shared CV with me, and Zian took path planning. We didn't appoint a single technical lead or a formal project manager---the brief devolves those choices to the team, and nobody pushed for the role. That was a decision by omission, and it cost us later. Table~2 maps what actually happened against Tuckman's (1965) stages.

The most dangerous row is the gap between Forming and Storming. Our Forming lasted one meeting. In Lencioni's (2002) terms, we divided tasks but never committed to a cadence for re-evaluating that division when circumstances changed. When the environment \emph{did} change---three cancelled flight days shifted all progress from the hardware bench to my laptop---the structure drifted silently because we had no routine for revisiting it. Unexplicit structure is a luxury that only survives in slack-rich teams, and ours had no slack.

The hardware situation deserves honest mention. All other teams had working hardware from the start. Ours wasn't functional until Easter. The first two practice flight days effectively didn't count---we arrived with scripts ready to run, a simulation to validate, a progressive test ladder prepared, and the hardware just didn't work. That's three flight days lost to equipment we didn't provide and couldn't control. We wrote a complaint letter about it, iterated the tone six times across all five team members, and sent it to the course organiser. The letter wasn't blame---it was a factual record of what we couldn't test and why.

\textbf{2b.}~The most impactful team-working element, measured by what it unblocked, was documentation that traded my time for Robin's. The four hours I spent writing the vision pipeline handoff doc and the \texttt{--robin} integration flag bought Robin roughly twenty hours of unblocked integration in wk20. He wired my detection output into his mission script without asking a single clarifying question. I now understand that worked because it traded solo time for onboarding time at roughly 5:1---better team economics than I'd assumed documentation ever delivered.

The wk17 FSM overlap needs to be on record. I'd built a complex state machine with timeout guards and a geofence repulsor. Robin argued it was over-engineered for a team of five with limited testing time. My first reaction was defensive---the FSM was \emph{my} code, and what felt like technical conviction was partly territorial reflex. Using Lencioni's pyramid, I was ring-fencing work because I didn't trust the team to navigate it. I took a day, reread Robin's flight script, and proposed a compromise: keep the full FSM for autonomous mode, strip it to lightweight HTTP polling for Robin's integration. He accepted. The integration took an afternoon instead of a week.

Robin and I were the two proactive members. Edward and Zian delivered assigned work competently but didn't drive things forward independently. \textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: is this fair? But it meant that unassigned work defaulted to two people, and that asymmetry shaped everything from workload to morale.

\textbf{2c.}~Three changes, each falsifiable. First: a weekly one-line decision log from wk1---every call that binds more than one person's work gets written down. The silent structural drift after wk16 would have been caught by any written record. Second: interface contracts \emph{before} parallel work begins. A single wk14 session defining the HTTP contract between my vision output and Robin's state machine would have prevented the wk17 overlap entirely. Third: stop treating documentation as a substitute for conversation. The two pieces of writing that actually changed behaviour were the short pivot message and the complaint letter---both brief, timely, paired with a live discussion. Any document over 500 words gets a 15-minute walkthrough within a week, or I treat it as unsent.
