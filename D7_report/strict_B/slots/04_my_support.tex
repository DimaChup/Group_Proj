Robin's wk17 pushback is the episode that matters most. What I'd framed as a technical disagreement was actually a maintainability one, and I preferred the technical framing because it protected me from noticing I'd built something only I could read. In Lencioni's (2002) terms, I was ring-fencing work because I didn't trust the team to navigate it --- and that looked like technical conviction when it was territorial reflex. I proposed a compromise: keep the full FSM for autonomous mode, strip it to HTTP polling for Robin's script. He accepted. Integration took an afternoon instead of a week.

Robin and I were the two proactive members of the team. We worked in close coordination on the CV-to-state-machine integration, and ideas were discussed between us as colleagues --- he'd suggest a concept like the repulsive force, I'd take it and develop it, neither of us treated it as a transaction. That dynamic was the closest thing to real engineering collaboration I experienced on the project.

The Robin handoff deserves scrutiny. The \texttt{passive\_watch} HTTP interface traded my solo time for his onboarding time at roughly 5:1 --- four hours writing docs and building the flag bought him twenty hours of unblocked work. I hadn't believed documentation delivered that kind of return. It worked because it gave Robin everything he needed without requiring him to understand my internals. That's the line between capability (I can build it) and enablement (someone else can use it without me) --- and I didn't cross it until wk20.

During the failed practice days, the whole team felt demotivated --- me included. I had loads of scripts ready to test and nowhere to test them. Instead of just sitting with the frustration, I went deeper into the simulation, running it thousands of times, modelling camera blur, thinking through everything that could go wrong. The simulation gave the team something to show when hardware couldn't, and it gave Robin's state machine a testbed that didn't require a drone in the air.

\textbf{What I leave believing.} I came as a Ukrainian-trained engineer whose reflex under failure was ``one person delivers.'' I leave believing its near-inversion: \emph{the engineer who delivers alone is one recruitment away from being the single point of failure in whatever she builds.} The wk17 conflict and Robin's wk20 unaided handoff told me, in different registers, that the same work which delivered the report was the work that kept my teammates at the edge of their own project. Falsifiable internship signal: in my first six weeks, does at least one teammate modify a module I own without asking permission? If yes, even once, I've started to become an engineer whose work enables other people's work. That's the only kind of engineering I now think is worth aspiring to, and I haven't resolved it yet.
