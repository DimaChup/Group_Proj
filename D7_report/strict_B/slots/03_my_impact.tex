The Kolb (1984) cycle ran deliberately, not retrospectively. \emph{Concrete experience}: three collapsed flight days --- practice days 1 and 2 produced zero usable flight time because we were given faulty hardware, and all the other teams had working hardware from the start. \emph{Reflective observation}: the team's definition of progress assumed hardware access we didn't have. Four of us were idle because planned work needed a drone in the air; I was blocked too, but I had a laptop. That asymmetry was the data point. \emph{Abstract conceptualisation}: if \texttt{vision.py} exposed a single interface (\texttt{detect\_in\_image} $\to$ \texttt{(found, x, y, conf)}) and auto-selected between Ultralytics on laptop and TFLite on Pi, then the same state machine could run in both places. Anything proven in simulation would, in principle, run on hardware. \emph{Active experimentation}: six weeks building on that premise. When we finally ran on the Pi, the delta was thin --- a bounding-box coordinate bug, a double-scaling fix, a deleted calibration file --- and the system came up.

But the sober closing: the simulation couldn't tell us what it couldn't tell us. It couldn't predict motion blur at 5\,m/s with a real IMX296 --- I only modelled Gaussian shake, not directional smear. It couldn't find the descent stall I accepted as a SITL artefact at 4--5\,m. And it couldn't reveal what GPS timing lag (100--200\,ms latency, $\approx$1\,m error at 5\,m/s) would do to a multi-pass lock. Sim-to-real parity is an argument I can only win about the things I modelled.

Against R01--R12, our system hit 12/12 in simulation but only \textcolor{red}{[X/12 --- Apollo to confirm actual number]} on real hardware by flight day. Some of those gaps were bench-test items we didn't schedule --- coordination failure, not engineering failure. That distinction matters: I mistook technical delivery for team enablement.

\textbf{3b.}~The metric I didn't track until too late: the number of times a teammate other than me edited \texttt{simple\_simulator.py} or \texttt{main.py} was zero. That's the gap. Three mechanisms for future projects, each with a test:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Every new tool must have a named teammate beneficiary documented \emph{before} it ships. If I can't name one, I don't start.
\item No file over 500 lines gets refactored solo. I pair on the review, and the signal is whether the partner can edit independently within 48 hours.
\item Two hours per week of explicit teaching, graded not by whether I explained but by whether the teammate completes a task within 48 hours without asking me a second question.
\end{enumerate}

The simulation was the right call --- it kept the project alive when hardware failed us. But the fact that it lived entirely in my head, and nobody else could maintain it, is a design flaw in how I work, not a strength. I want to resolve that tension in my \textcolor{red}{[internship / next role --- Apollo to specify]}.
