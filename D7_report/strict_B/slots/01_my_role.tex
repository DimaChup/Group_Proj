\section{My role}

I want to open with the thing I'm still not comfortable admitting. At the wk12 kickoff I was given a narrow role: train a YOLOv8n dummy detector and hand it to the flight team. By wk20 I owned the state machine, the simulator, the geofence, the GPS estimation pipeline, 127 unit tests, and the D6 bibliography. Nobody asked me to take any of that on. \textbf{1a.}~The explicit allocation was computer vision --- model training, \texttt{vision.py} as a standalone module, TFLite export for the Pi. The implicit pickup happened one commit at a time: whenever something sat between the team and a working demo and nobody else was reaching for it, I reached. The shift crystallised around wk16, after our second flight day was cancelled and the project's centre of gravity moved from the hardware bench to my laptop --- because that was the only surface where code could still run. I didn't announce this; it just happened. I initially framed the drift as diligence. I now see it was partly avoidance: building infrastructure alone felt productive during the roughly eight weeks when three cancelled flight days left us with zero real flight data, and it protected me from the harder skill of slowing down so a teammate could catch up. My Ukrainian engineering training says one person delivers. This project said that's a failure mode dressed as a strength.

\textbf{1b.}~What I'm proudest of isn't the retrained model at mAP50\,=\,0.995, or the 820-plus commits, or the D6 score climb from 74 to 85.2 across six scoring cycles. It's the first cancelled flight day (2026-03-11). The weather killed our only window. Instead of going home I calibrated the focal length to 5.46\,mm against a tape measure, ran the checkerboard lens calibration, wrote \texttt{FIELD\_QUICK\_REF.md} for no-internet field use, and closed ten commits before dark. That day crystallised my thesis about this project: \emph{the simulation framework we built ourselves is the only reason this project produced any verifiable engineering output at all.} What I'm proud of is refusing to let a missing drone become a missing report. Had I acted on my ``build a simulator first'' instinct a week earlier, we'd have saved five days of idle waiting --- a pattern I now recognise in myself: I trust the escape route too late.

The BGR camera debugging (2026-02-17) is the incident I want to name as reflection-in-action (Sch\"on, 1983). I spent hours on AWB modes, manual gains, ISP tuning, a kernel update --- all wrong. Then I stopped guessing and systematically tested all six channel permutations. Root cause: the IMX296 outputs BGR despite labelling itself RGB888. One reading of that night is ``good debugging discipline.'' Another is that I burned three hours on intuition-driven fixes before switching to systematic diagnosis, and that ratio tells me something about how I respond to frustration --- I reach for plausible explanations instead of exhaustive ones, and the switch only happens after frustration has built up enough to override the comfort of guessing.

\textbf{1c.}~The distinction I will focus on in future work is between \emph{capability} and \emph{enablement}. I leave this project believing engineering leadership is less about being the one who fixes the geofence sign-convention bug at midnight and more about making my fix legible enough that the module owner would've found it without me. The evidence: the \texttt{passive\_watch\_2.py} handoff to Robin (commit \texttt{a5b1ab7}). I built the \texttt{--robin} flag and \texttt{cv\_mode} polling interface so his state machine could consume my vision output over HTTP without touching my code. Robin then integrated for two days without asking me a single question. That was the first teamwork move I made on this project that scaled without my presence --- and it happened in wk20, not wk12. In my June robotics internship, I'll schedule two hours per week of explicit pair-programming and handoff review. The falsifiable signal: can a teammate complete a handoff task independently within 48 hours? If not, my documentation has failed, not their attention.
