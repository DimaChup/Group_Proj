## A.2 History of Health & Safety Legislation

### A.2.1 Pre-industrial context

Before the Industrial Revolution, most people earned a living through agriculture or small-scale domestic manufacture. Industrialisation introduced a fundamentally new class of hazard: powerful machinery, long hours, and chemical exposure -- risks qualitatively different from "falling off a horse or felling a tree." The workforce routinely included young children.

### A.2.2 Timeline of key legislation

| Year | Legislation / Event | Key Provisions | Weakness |
|------|---------------------|----------------|----------|
| **1802** | Health and Morals of Apprentices Act ("Factory Act") | Created by factory owner and MP **Sir Robert Peel**. Prevented apprentices (up to age 21) from working at night or more than 12 hours/day. Provisions for clothing and accommodation. Textile industry had 20,000 apprentices, ~1/5 under 13. | **No legal powers of enforcement.** |
| **1833** | Factory Act (Second) | Driven by the "10 Hour Movement" campaign. No child under 9 could work in factories. Ages 9-13: max 8 hours/day. Ages 13-18: max 12 hours/day. | Only **4 inspectors** for all **4,000 textile mills** in Britain. |
| **1835** | *Priestley v Fowler* | First recorded **duty of care** case. Charles Priestley was delivering a wagon of mutton; the cart rolled over, breaking his thigh and dislocating his shoulder. Remained at a local inn for 19 weeks; stay cost 50 pounds (~5,000 pounds today). Sued employer, awarded 100 pounds. **Landmark: established that employers owe employees a duty of care.** | Case law only, not statute. |
| **1842** | Mines Act | Extended protections to mining. | Narrow scope. |
| **1880** | Employers' Liability Act | First **statute** making employers legally liable for workplace injuries. | -- |
| **1974** | **Health and Safety at Work Act (HSAWA)** | See Section A.4 below. Overarching legislation, still in force today. | -- |
| **2007** | Corporate Manslaughter and Corporate Homicide Act | Enables prosecution of **organisations** (not just individuals). See Section A.7. | -- |

### A.2.3 Industrial hazards -- specific examples from the lectures

- **White phosphorus in matchmaking factories:** Added to match heads to improve burning. Caused "phossy jaw" (jawbone necrosis, teeth fall out). Fatal in ~20% of cases. Banned elsewhere from 1870; continued in Britain until **1910**.
- **Glassworks:** Workers (including children) routinely burnt or blinded.
- **Child labour in textiles:** 20,000 apprentices, many under 13, operating dangerous machinery (slide s05 -- Hine photograph of boys climbing spinning equipment).

## A.3 Key Event: Flixborough Disaster (1 June 1974)

> The single most important catalyst for the Health and Safety at Work Act.

| Detail | Fact |
|--------|------|
| Location | Nypro UK chemical plant, Flixborough, North Lincolnshire |
| Product | Caprolactam (nylon feedstock), using cyclohexane through 6 reactors |
| Root cause | Reactor 5 found damaged. Reactors 4 and 6 joined with **temporary piping** (to avoid lengthy shutdown). Temporary piping **failed**. |
| Leak | ~20-40 tonnes of cyclohexane gas |
| Explosion | Massive fuel-air explosion when gas cloud reached ignition source |
| Deaths | **28 total**: 18 in plant control room (immediate), 9 other site workers, 1 delivery driver (heart attack) |
| Damage | **~2,000 buildings** damaged by shockwaves |
| Fire duration | 10 days |
| Timing factor | Happened on a **weekend**; estimated **~500 deaths** if on a weekday |
| Legislative impact | HSAWA was already under development but Flixborough **accelerated its implementation** |

## A.4 The Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 (HSAWA)

> "The primary piece of legislation covering occupational health and safety in Great Britain." (Slide s09)

### Core principle

> "Duty upon employers to ensure, **as far as is reasonably practicable**, the health, safety and welfare of employees."

### Scope

- Encompasses **all industries** (not just factories)
- Sets out duties of:
  - Employers towards employees **and** members of the public
  - Employees to **themselves and to each other**
  - Certain self-employed workers towards themselves and others

### Critical legal point

> **"No one has to be harmed for an offence to be committed under the Act -- there only has to be a risk of harm."**

### Six key employer duties (from slide s11 -- Section 2 extract)

1. **Provide plant, equipment and systems** that work and are safe
2. **Ensure safe handling** of substances and materials
3. **Deliver adequate training**, instruction and supervision
4. **Provide a safe place of work** with safe access and welfare facilities
5. **Develop and maintain a written Health and Safety Policy**
6. **Consult with workplace safety representatives** (particularly trade union reps)

> **Note:** Employers also have a duty of care for employees' **mental health**, not just physical health.

## A.8 ALARP and VPF

### ALARP -- As Low As Reasonably Practicable

The phrase "reasonably practicable" in HSAWA requires employers to perform a **cost-benefit analysis** when considering risks. The expression ALARP is used commonly in the **rail industry**, which has a detailed framework for analysing the cost of risks, including fatalities.

> *"In most cases there are no hard and fast rules about what is reasonably practical and often it is reliant on the experience of company safety experts to know what risks are deemed appropriate."*

Slide s14 shows the HSAWA Section 2 text with **"reasonably practicable"** highlighted repeatedly -- appearing 7+ times in the section.

### VPF -- Value of a Prevented Fatality

| Year | VPF |
|------|-----|
| 2016 | **1.83 million pounds** per fatality |

Published annually by the Department for Transport.

**Application example:** A network operator might reasonably spend **18.3 million pounds** on risk mitigations to prevent a theoretical **10 fatalities** on the rail network.

> *"Would you spend 5,000,000 to save 20 lives or 5,000,000 to save 50 lives? You'd spend 5,000,000 to save 50 lives. Of course, you instinctively perform a benefit calculation."*

VPF allows companies to **compare cost-benefit ratios** of different safety investments and yield the best overall safety with available funds.

> *"Is that good enough? Well, no, not really. 150 fatalities is still a very high number."*

## A.10 Your Role as an Engineer

> "Safety is **everyone's responsibility** and your professional approach to working safely **starts now**."

Key responsibilities:
- **Report possible hazards** to the person responsible for managing the site
- **Don't walk by** -- if you see something wrong, report it
- **If someone else is doing something risky**, make their manager or supervisor aware
- Behave as professionally at university as you plan to in employment
- Getting the basics right now protects you and those around you

> *"You might just save their life. They might not have noticed that what they are doing is dangerous."*

## B.2 What Is Risk?

### Etymology
The word "risk" derives from the early Italian *risicare*, meaning **"to dare."** In that sense, risk is more of a **choice** than fate alone.

### Historical context
Peter Bernstein's book *Against the Gods* argues that humanity's changed approach to risk is what separates modern from ancient times. Before the Renaissance, people relied on fate, mysticism, and the gods. Thinkers like **Pascal and Fermat** developed theories of probability -- inspired not by safety but by **gambling at dice**.

> *"The art of risk management, for it is an art, is the most important activity of any senior manager in a technical field."*

### ISO 31000 definition

> **Risk is "the effect of uncertainty on objectives."**
> -- ISO 31000:2018, *Risk Management -- Guidelines*

ISO and BS standards accessible free through University of Bristol library at: `http://www.bristol.ac.uk/library/find/standards/`

## B.5 Mitigations: Reduce Likelihood, Reduce Consequence, or Both

### The climbing example (from slides s05-s06)

| Mitigation | Reduces Likelihood? | Reduces Consequence? |
|------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Climbing Helmet** | No (X) -- but see risk compensation below | Yes -- protects your head |
| **Rope** | Yes -- less chance of falling to ground | Yes -- breaks your fall, less injury |

> **Key takeaway:** Different mitigations can improve the **likelihood**, **consequence**, or **both**.

---

## B.6 Risk Compensation

> *"Could the helmet affect the likelihood of falling? Yes, it could actually. The climber might think they are safer because they have a helmet and be less careful about holding on."*

Real-world example from the lecture:

> *"Electricians seem increasingly careless when operating on live circuits in your house. I think this is because they're overconfident in the performance of modern circuit breakers and they think that the risk of electric shock is so low that they no longer need to worry about it. And I think that increases the likelihood of having an electric shock, even if the consequence might still be limited."*

This is the phenomenon where the **presence of a safety mitigation causes behavioural changes** that partially offset the safety benefit.

## B.7 HSE Five Steps to Risk Assessment

Adapted from HSE guidance (slide s07). The process is **cyclic** -- risk assessments should be reviewed on a regular basis.

```
    +---> 1. Identify hazards
    |         |
    |     2. Assess the risks
    |         |
    |     3. Control the risks
    |         |
    |     4. Record your findings
    |         |
    +---- 5. Review the controls
```

> *"This assessment requires an update by [date]"* -- ensures documents are kept current.

> *"Having a look at the risk assessments is one of the key things that an auditor does when they come round and visit your company."*

## B.8 Risk Assessment Pro Forma -- 9 Columns Explained

The standard pro forma has 9 columns (from slides s08-s10):

| # | Column | Purpose |
|---|--------|---------|
| 1 | **Date and Initials of reviewer** | Traceability. Shows when risk was last reviewed. |
| 2 | **Type of Risk** | Classification: Safety, Schedule, Financial, Environmental, Legal, etc. |
| 3 | **Description of Hazard** | Short narrative of what might happen. E.g., "hitting my hand" when hammering a nail. |
| 4 | **Description of Consequence** | Primary consequences. E.g., "injury." Secondary consequences are harder to handle in basic RAs. |
| 5 | **Frequency (f)** | Likelihood score (1-5). See B.9. |
| 6 | **Consequence (c)** | Consequence score (1-5). See B.10. |
| 7 | **Risk Number (f x c)** | Product of frequency and consequence. Used to rank, prioritise, or gate projects. |
| 8 | **Mitigation** | Preventative or reactive actions. *"Be careful -- mitigations may cause unwanted additional risks."* |
| 9 | **Residual Risk** | Risk that **remains** even after mitigations are deployed. Can be a verbal comment or a re-scored f x c. |

> **Best practice:** Risk assessment should be done as a **multidisciplinary team** -- "working alone can cause you to miss things." Non-experts can also add value: *"they often ask great questions... they might notice things that you hadn't noticed before."*

Other risk assessment types mentioned on slide: Risk Register, **DFMEA** (Design), **PFMEA** (Process), **IFMEA** (Interface), **FMECA** (Failure Mode, Effects, and Criticality Analysis).

## B.10 Consequence Scoring (1-5, Network Rail Examples)

| Score | Classification | Safety Description | Performance Description |
|-------|---------------|--------------------|------------------------|
| **1** | **Minimal** | Minor safety event; potential cause up to 28 days off work; addressed using existing control measures | Minor impact |

## B.11 Risk Number = Frequency x Consequence

> The resulting number **guides you as to what to do**. Risk numbers can be used to:
> - **Rank** risks
> - **Prioritise** actions
> - **Prevent a project from progressing** if the number is high enough

Most companies have requirements for managing risks above a certain threshold.

### Treatment guidance (colour-coded on slide)

| Risk Number | Classification | Action |
|-------------|---------------|--------|
| **1-5** (green) | **Negligible** | Existing control measures are likely appropriate. |
| **6-10** (amber) | **Tolerable** | Additional control measures and mitigations required. Written action plan required. |
| **11-25** (red) | **Intolerable** | Activity must **halt**. Resumption requires **approval from the safety board**. |

## B.13 Residual Risk

> *"The residual risk is the risk that remains even after I've deployed my mitigations. Most mitigations don't remove the risk altogether."*

**Climbing rope example:** The rope lowers both the frequency and consequence of a fall, but not to zero. "Perhaps the climber still falls but is injured rather than being killed."

In more developed risk assessments, residual risk is captured by **re-scoring** frequency and consequence after mitigations. For basic assessments, a verbal comment suffices.

# PART D -- Application to SAR Drone Project

## D.15 Risk Management Applied to the SAR Drone

### D.15.1 Project requirements (R01-R12) as risk boundaries

The AENGM0074 project brief defines 12 requirements (R01-R12) that function as **risk boundaries**. Each requirement implicitly addresses a category of risk:

| Requirement | Risk Type | What it controls |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| R01: Flight Area boundary | Safety | Prevents drone from leaving the defined operational area -- reduces likelihood of third-party harm |
| R02: SSSI No-Fly Zone | Environmental / Legal | Prevents disturbance to a Site of Special Scientific Interest |
| R03: Take-off from designated point | Safety | Ensures known launch conditions, distance verification at arming |
| R04: Maximum 50m altitude | Safety / Legal | Hard cap prevents high-altitude incidents and airspace violations |
| R07: Land near casualty | Performance | Mission objective -- the "consequence" of not finding the target |
| R10: Record detection evidence | Quality | Provides audit trail, enables post-mission review |
| R12: Open-source licence | Legal | MIT licence compliance |

### D.15.2 Mitigations mapped to the Likelihood/Consequence framework

Applying Graham's climbing analogy directly to our system:

| SAR Drone Mitigation | Reduces Likelihood? | Reduces Consequence? | Equivalent in lecture |
|----------------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **Software geofence** (cv2.pointPolygonTest, emergency RTL) | Yes -- prevents entering NFZ | Yes -- auto-return limits exposure time | Rope |
| **RC kill switch** (pilot can switch to STABILIZE at any time) | No -- hazard still occurs | Yes -- immediate manual control stops autonomous behaviour | Helmet |
| **Progressive test ladder** (bench -> passive -> waypoint -> auto) | Yes -- catches bugs before they reach flight | Yes -- each step limits the severity of failure | Climbing training |
| **50m altitude hard cap** (navigation.py) | Yes -- prevents high-altitude operation | Yes -- lower altitude = lower kinetic energy on impact | Rope anchor |
| **Operator verification** (Y/N confirmation before landing) | Yes -- prevents false-positive landings | No -- does not change what happens if it does land wrong | -- |
| **ALARP confidence threshold** (0.4 default, tuneable) | Yes -- filters noise | Risk compensation effect -- lower threshold catches more targets but increases false positives | Helmet (risk compensation) |

### D.15.3 Risk compensation in our project

Graham warns about risk compensation: the helmet makes you less careful. In our project:

- **NCNN giving 4.5x faster inference** (72ms vs 327ms) could tempt the team to fly faster, reducing detection window per frame -- the speed improvement makes us *feel* safer and therefore potentially less cautious about flight speed.
- **Successful simulation** can create overconfidence about real-world performance. Our simulation scores 7.7/10 but real hardware scored only 2.1/10 at assessment time.

### D.15.4 Three failed flight days as risk materialisation

Three scheduled outdoor flight days were cancelled due to weather. Applying the risk framework:

| Risk | Type | f | c | f x c | Mitigation | Residual |
|------|------|---|---|-------|------------|----------|
| Weather cancels flight day | Schedule | 4 (Likely -- UK weather) | 3 (Moderate -- delays project by days/weeks) | **12 (Intolerable)** | Book multiple flight days; have indoor bench-test plan as fallback | Still dependent on weather; project deadline is fixed |

This is a textbook **schedule risk** that materialised. The mitigation (booking multiple days) was applied, and bench testing on the Pi during the cancelled day still produced valuable data (FOV calibration, lens calibration, benchmark results).

### D.15.5 ALARP applied to our project decisions

> *"Risks have to be balanced against opportunities"* -- Graham

| Decision | Risk accepted | Benefit gained | ALARP justification |
|----------|--------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Using TFLite (206ms) instead of waiting for NCNN optimisation | Slower detection = missed targets at high speed | Ship on time; TFLite is proven stable | Reasonably practicable -- NCNN untested on Pi at decision time |
| 35m search altitude (not 20m) | Smaller target in frame, lower detection confidence | Wider FOV = faster area coverage, fewer passes needed | Cost-benefit: halving altitude quadruples the number of passes |
| Single-class model (not 5-class) | Cannot distinguish dummy pose/clothing | Simpler, faster, proven mAP50=0.995 | Multi-class adds complexity for marginal real-world benefit |

## D.17 Hatton L4 Moves Enabled

To hit L4 on the Hatton marking scheme (critical evaluation / synthesis), use these patterns:

| L4 Move | How to execute | Example sentence |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Apply theory to own project with specific evidence** | Map Graham's 5-step process to your actual risk register | "Following the HSE five-step process (Graham, 2023), we identified 12 project hazards, scored them using a 5x5 criticality matrix, and implemented software-enforced mitigations including a runtime geofence (R01) that triggers emergency RTL." |
| **Critique the framework** | Note limitations of the 5x5 matrix for software-intensive systems | "The standard criticality matrix assumes independent risks, but in our system the GPS-loss hazard (f=2, c=5) cascades into both geofence failure and navigation failure, compounding the effective risk number beyond the simple f x c product." |
| **Connect moral/economic/legal pillars to concrete decisions** | Show how all three pillars influenced a single decision | "The decision to implement RC override guard was driven by all three pillars: morally, the pilot must always be able to protect bystanders; economically, a crash would destroy approximately 2,000 pounds of equipment; legally, the drone operator bears personal liability under the Air Navigation Order 2016." |
| **Use ALARP with quantitative reasoning** | Show the cost-benefit trade-off numerically | "Reducing search altitude from 35m to 20m would increase detection confidence by an estimated 15% but would require 3.1x more passes to cover the same area, extending mission time from 8 to 25 minutes -- a schedule risk increase from f=2 to f=4 that we judged not reasonably practicable." |
| **Identify risk compensation in your own work** | Self-critical analysis | "The successful simulation (7.7/10 capability score) arguably created risk compensation within the team: confidence in simulation reduced perceived urgency of hardware testing, which was then blocked by three weather cancellations -- a compounding of schedule and performance risk." |
| **Show iterative risk management** | Evidence that risks were re-assessed | "After field testing revealed that calibration_data.npz was corrupt (causing detection failure), we re-scored the 'AI fails to detect' hazard from f=2 to f=4 and added a pre-flight model validation step to our checklist." |
