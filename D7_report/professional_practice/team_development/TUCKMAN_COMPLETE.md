## 1. Why Teams? Benefits and Drawbacks

Mark opens by establishing why humans work in groups before introducing the model. This is not filler -- it frames the *context* Tuckman was responding to.

### Benefits of Teams

| Benefit | Detail (from transcript) |
|---|---|
| **Wider knowledge access** | "Access to a much wider range of knowledge and information... can help accelerate problem solving" |
| **Collective courage** | "Safety in numbers feeling... teams will often undertake challenges that no individual would consider doing alone" |
| **Interaction and creativity** | "Being in a group does create new opportunities for interaction and creativity and accidental discoveries" |
| **Greater than sum of parts** | "Good teams should add up to more than the sum of their parts" |

### Drawbacks and Risks

| Risk | Detail (from transcript) |
|---|---|
| **Groupthink** | "Being surrounded by people who think just like you creates an echo chamber and the problem of groupthink" -- caused by "managers recruiting clones of themselves" |
| **Conformance pressure** | "Increase in the social pressure towards conformance and assimilating the views of the group" |
| **Extroverts vs introverts** | "Extroverts speak to think... introverts tend to think to speak -- these differences might require differences in the way that the team is run" |
| **Democratic weakness** | "Solutions that are converged upon might be the ones that have the greatest average approval... rather than the best ideas available. Maybe in politics, just because it's popular doesn't make it good or right" |
| **Dominant personalities** | "Dominant personalities in the group exerting an unequal impact on the outcomes. Possibly that dominant character might even be you" |
| **Diffusion of responsibility** | "People were relying on the group to manage the risk and no one realised that no one else was reading the map either" (hiking accident analogy) |
| **Lack of intellectual diversity** | "Teams of people that seem to lack any intellectual diversity" |

### Key Quote
> "Some of the worst acts in history have been as a result of the will of the people."

## 2. Team Time Allocation Model

Mark presents a "crude model" showing how time splits between technical work and team management. This is the slide with 4 bar charts.

### The Four Scenarios

| Scenario | Technical Work | Team Management | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Working alone** | ~100% | ~0% | "Nice and simple. No one else to worry about. But hard to find a job that fulfils that desire, pays well, and has any security" |
| **Typical team** | ~50% | ~50% | "Maybe 50% of the team's time can be spent managing the team... Already this seems like a heavy burden" |
| **Poor team** | <50% | >50% | "Really poor teams often spend the majority of their time managing the team because its culture or processes are so broken" |
| **Ideal team** | >50% | <50% | "Teams that invest in managing themselves well will have to spend less time managing themselves in the end" |

**The irony** (Mark's key insight): Teams that *hate* talking about management and skip it end up spending *more* time on management. Teams that invest early spend *less*.

> "Teams that don't bother to think about how they work, possibly because they hate talking about management, will have to spend even more time trying to manage the team."

### Worker vs Manager Time Split

| Role | Own Technical Work | Team/People Work |
|---|---|---|
| **Worker** | High (~85-90%) | Low (daily standup ~30 min) |
| **Mid-level manager** (5-30 staff) | ~50% | ~50% |
| **Senior manager** | Very little | Almost everything |

> "As a senior manager... it can often feel like you never get to work on your own work. The reality is that the team's work *is* your work."

Mark warns: the "forced detachment from the technical work which might have been the reason why you entered engineering, can be too much to bear in the long term."

## 3. The Tuckman Model -- Overview

### Historical Context

- **1960s**: Increasing scale of industrial organisations required more effective human factors management
- **1965**: American psychologist **Bruce Tuckman** published *"Developmental Sequence in Small Groups"* in *Psychological Bulletin*, 63(6), 384-399
- He studied teams and noticed that "as groups assembled and began working together, the group seemed to all transition through a series of common stages"
- **1977**: Tuckman and **Mary Ann Jensen** added a fifth stage (adjourning/transforming) in *"Stages of Small-Group Development Revisited"*, Group & Organization Studies, 2(4), 419-427

### The Two Axes (from transcript)

Mark describes the chart as having:
- **X-axis**: "Time, task, and function" (left to right)
- **Y-axis**: "Personal relationships and feelings within the team" (bottom to top)

### Tuckman's Original Two Dimensions (from research)

Tuckman actually separated group functioning into two parallel realms:

| Dimension | Forming | Storming | Norming | Performing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Group Structure** (interpersonal) | Testing & Dependence | Intragroup Conflict | Development of Cohesion | Functional Role-Relatedness |
| **Task Activity** | Orientation to Task | Emotional Response to Task Demands | Open Exchange of Interpretations | Emergence of Solutions |

The correspondence between these two realms over time produced the four-stage summary: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing.

### The Five Stages at a Glance

| Stage | Personal Relations | Task/Function | Key Word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Dependency | Orienting | Polite |
| **Storming** | Tension | Organising | Conflict |
| **Norming** | Cohesion | Sharing | Belonging |
| **Performing** | Inter-dependence | Solving | Fluid |
| **Transforming** | Disengagement | Transitioning | Loss |

> "As with all good models, it's simple enough to remember, but sophisticated enough to be useful."

## 5. Stage 1: Forming

**Personal Relations:** Dependency
**Task Function:** Orienting

### From the Transcript

- People "act independently without any real sense of purpose and direction"
- "People will be very polite and show some goodwill to others"
- "Trust will be limited and there will be a mix of excitement and anxiety"
- Discussions focus on "what the team has been formed for, the common purpose and how the team will work together -- ground rules and expectations"
- "There will not yet be a clear or common understanding of the problem or the tasks necessary to develop the solution"
- "The team will be looking for leadership, but that will not yet be clear"
- "There'll be lots of questions, and many of these questions might just be trivial, with some questions being asked just as a way of the team getting to know each other"

### Surprise: Not Actually Unpleasant

> "At the very beginning of projects, it's not normally the hardest or most unpleasant phase. At this point, everyone's mostly friendly and the team's deliverables are a long way off in the future, so there's not much pressure on them to deliver yet."

### What to Do as a Leader (from research)

- Provide clear direction and purpose
- Establish ground rules explicitly
- Create opportunities for members to get to know each other
- Define roles early, even if they shift later
- Keep expectations realistic

---

## 6. Stage 2: Storming

**Personal Relations:** Tension
**Task Function:** Organising

### From the Transcript

- "Ideas start to come from individuals. Rivals may emerge and compete for different work packages or roles"
- "Levels of politeness drop as individuals start to realise that the clock is ticking"
- "Individual differences and ways of working start to be pronounced as team members start to show their true selves"
- "Sometimes this can result in conflict"
- "Opinions about others start to be shared with them, either directly or indirectly"
- "Teams might just try to find some democratic approaches to find peace, but this can often just push issues underground rather than actually addressing them"
- "The team often realises that the initial goals are too big or too abstract to be useful to actually get the work done, and that new, more granular goals are needed"
- "The team is also starting to find out what methods work and what methods fail"

### Mark's Universal Claim

> "Without exception, every new team I've worked within or have managed has experienced this stage, and the length of this stage can be influenced by a great many factors, including cultural differences or pressures coming in from other projects."

### Key Danger: Underground Conflict

Democratic approaches to "find peace" can push issues underground. The conflict does not go away -- it just becomes invisible and toxic. Better to surface and address it.

### What to Do as a Leader (from research)

- Normalise conflict -- it is expected and healthy
- Facilitate open discussion of disagreements
- Break abstract goals into concrete, actionable tasks
- Clarify roles and responsibilities explicitly
- Do not suppress storming -- it must be worked through

---

## 7. Stage 3: Norming

**Personal Relations:** Cohesion
**Task Function:** Sharing

### From the Transcript

- "A sense of belonging should develop and personality clashes are less likely"
- "Decision making is easier because roles and responsibilities have become clearer"
- "Information flows between members more frequently and more easily"
- "Constructive criticism even becomes possible and maybe even welcome from group members"
- "Being part of the team here may actually be enjoyable"
- "Useful and detailed plans should start to appear"
- "A team starts to develop its own culture, possibly with its own language and its own in-jokes"

### Indicators You Have Reached Norming

1. Criticism is constructive, not personal
2. Team has inside jokes or shared vocabulary
3. Plans are detailed and realistic (not abstract wishlists)
4. Information flows freely without being asked for
5. Members feel a sense of belonging

---

## 8. Stage 4: Performing

**Personal Relations:** Inter-dependence
**Task Function:** Solving

### From the Transcript

- "The sweet spot and the target for your team. You want to spend as much time as possible here"
- "The team is delivering high quality outcomes and showing a good level of efficiency"
- "Team members will have knowledge of their own and other strengths and weaknesses"
- "The team will be able to say that they are able to solve problems"
- "Roles may become much more fluid. Moving work around becomes quite natural"
- "Differences between team members become useful rather than troublesome"
- "The team is now making significant progress against its goals and levels of commitment within the team are high"
- "Progress is recognised and celebrated by the team"

### Key Characteristic: Fluidity

At Performing, rigid role boundaries dissolve. Work flows to whoever can best handle it. This is fundamentally different from Forming, where roles are unclear (chaotic), versus Performing, where roles are flexible (intentional).

## 9. Stage 5: Transforming / Adjourning (1977)

**Personal Relations:** Disengagement
**Task Function:** Transitioning

### From the Transcript

- Added in 1977 by Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen
- "Looks at winding down the team and its activities"
- "Needs to be done well to manage the sense of loss and also to capture what the team has learnt from the project"
- "Some people have actually termed this stage mourning"

### Emotions (from transcript)

| Emotion | Example Quote |
|---|---|
| **Disbelief / Denial** | "Oh I can't believe the team's being split up" |
| **Depression / Morose** | "When you've invested years of your life into a project and someone pulls the plug on it, it can be really painful" |
| **Grief-like cycle** | "The types of emotions and feelings experienced can be related to the cycle of grief" |

### From Research

- Originally called "adjourning" by Tuckman and Jensen (1977)
- Some texts use "mourning" to emphasise the emotional dimension
- The White-Fairhurst TPR model later reframed "transforming" differently (grouping F-S-N as a single "transforming" phase)
- Mark uses "transforming" rather than "adjourning" in this lecture

## 10. Timeline Scenarios

Mark presents three scenarios showing where teams spend most of their time. "The model is definitely not linear" but these show patterns.

### Best Case (very, very rare)

```
|--F--|--S--|---------- PERFORMING ----------|--T--|
```

- Majority of time in Performing
- "This is what all project managers will optimistically base their timing plans on"
- Short Forming and Storming phases
- Mark emphasises: **very, very rare**

### Probable / Mostly Acceptable Case

```
|----F----|-------S-------|---N---|---P---|---T---|
```

- "Forming and storming stages are longer and that has the effect of compressing the performing aspect"
- "Due to human nature, the team will need more time to wind down without having an existential crisis"

### Worst Case (to avoid)

```
|F|--------STORMING--------|--F2--|--S2--|N|P|--T--|
```

- "Too little time spent forming, which means no building of trust in the team, no discussion of its aims or its goals, and just jumping straight into the storming phase"
- "Possibly then requires the team to start again with a reforming and a restorming after some strong external intervention"
- External intervention examples: "the swap out of a team member or the assignment of a new project to the team"

### Key Warning

> "Unless you're naturally gifted or lucky with your team members, you will have to actually actively manage the team to achieve the best case scenario."

## 11. Team Regression

Teams can move backwards through the stages.

### From the Transcript

> "You can go from right to left. Certainly your team could undevelop. This might happen if new members join or your project changes so significantly that your team is thrown back a few stages in its development."

### From Research

- A team happily Norming or Performing can regress to Storming when a new member joins
- The regression is usually not as dramatic as starting from scratch, but trust must be re-established
- New members bring their own working style, temporarily disrupting established norms
- The team may need to re-Storm and re-Form before returning to Performing
- Major project changes, leadership changes, or external shocks can also trigger regression

### Triggers for Regression

1. New team member joins
2. Team member leaves
3. Project scope changes significantly
4. Leadership change
5. External crisis or pressure
6. Loss of a key resource

## 12. Warning: Artificial Harmony

Mark issues a specific warning about teams that appear to skip Storming.

> "You might find that you breeze through because you as a team, you naturally gel with each other and you're all violently agreeing with each other all the time. Be careful of that."

### Why It Is Dangerous

- "Some level of conflict of ideas in the team is essential"
- "If there is no conflict of ideas, that suggests that you're all thinking the same thing"
- "Diversity of thought is critical in finding the best solutions"

### The Pattern

"Violently agreeing" = everyone nodding along = no diversity of thought = groupthink = suboptimal solutions. True Norming involves *resolved* conflict, not *absent* conflict.

## 13. Using the Model -- Practical Application

Mark closes with concrete advice on what to *do* with the model.

### Step 1: Diagnose

> "You can use this model to understand where you are. At what stage do you think your team is at? Think back over the behaviours and the activities I've mentioned for the different stages and identify any matches."

### Step 2: Advance

> "If you're stuck at a stage and feel that you should be closer to performing, you can ask yourself what can be done to get there faster. Can you engage in some different activities or ways of working as a team to get an improvement?"

### Step 3: Possible Actions

- "Setting clearer goals"
- "Revisiting some aspects of the forming stage that are needed to make a strong team"

### Step 4: Remember the Fractal Nature

> "Your team is part of a developing division and company. You will need to repeat some aspects of the Tuckman cycle at each new level of interface within the company. Your division is a team of teams and your company is another level of integration, each needing its own cycle to allow it to enter the performing stage."

## 16. Relationship to Other Models

| Model | Connection to Tuckman |
|---|---|
| **Belbin Team Roles** | Explains *why* Storming occurs -- role clashes (e.g., two Shapers competing). Performing requires role complementarity |
| **Kolb Experiential Learning** | Each Tuckman stage involves a learning cycle -- concrete experience (doing), reflective observation (what happened), abstract conceptualisation (why), active experimentation (try differently) |
| **Hatton Reflection Levels** | Reflecting *on* team development through Tuckman = L3 minimum. Critically evaluating Tuckman's applicability = L4 |
| **Gantt/WBS (PM2)** | Best-case Tuckman assumes short Storming -- project plans built on this are optimistic. Probable case means compressed Performing = less productive time than planned |
| **SWOT/Stakeholder Analysis** | Internal team SWOT should consider current Tuckman stage as a factor |

## 17. Limitations of the Tuckman Model (for critical reflection)

1. **Original study basis**: Tuckman reviewed literature on therapy groups, not workplace teams. He admitted the literature "cannot be considered truly representative of small-group developmental processes" (Tuckman, 1965)
2. **Linearity assumption**: The model implies sequential progression, but real teams oscillate. Tuckman acknowledged this but the model's visual representation encourages linear thinking
3. **No timeframes**: The model gives no indication of how long each stage should last. A team could be Storming for weeks or months
4. **Cultural bias**: Developed in 1960s American context. Collectivist cultures may exhibit different patterns (e.g., suppressed Storming that surfaces later)
5. **Team size**: Original research covered small groups. Applicability to large teams or teams-of-teams is extrapolated, not proven
6. **Pre-assigned vs self-selected**: University project teams are assigned, not chosen. This changes Forming dynamics significantly
7. **Remote/hybrid work**: The model predates remote work. Digital communication may alter how stages manifest
