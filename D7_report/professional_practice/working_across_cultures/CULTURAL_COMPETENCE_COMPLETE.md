## Learning Objectives (verbatim from lecture, 1:14--1:28)

1. Develop an awareness of our own personal culture and describe how that can affect the way we interpret situations.
2. Recall a range of key features of established cultural models and describe a simple development model for cultural competence.

## 1. Core Concept: "We See What We Believe"

Mark opens by inverting the common saying "believing is seeing" --- in reality, **"we see what we believe"** (2:31). This is the foundational idea of the entire lecture.

### The Mechanism

- When people experience an event, they do not respond to the **actual event**. They respond to the **meaning they attach** to that event (2:39--2:44).
- That meaning is heavily influenced by **prior experience** --- no two people attach exactly the same meaning to the same situation (2:50--2:57).
- The accumulated behaviours, habits, and values we develop constitute our **personal culture** (3:01--3:08).

### Unconscious Bias

The term Mark uses for this lens effect is **unconscious bias** (2:33--2:37). We are not aware of the filter through which we interpret events. The earlier in life we absorb a cultural norm, the more invisible it becomes --- "to the point that we tend to see the culture we experience as a child as just a reflection of what it is to be human" (4:44--4:55), and we may even label groups that think differently as "wrong or immoral" (4:55--5:00).

### Identity Groups

Culture is not just nationality. It is the **collective mental programming** (Hofstede's phrase, cited at 3:34) we absorb from every group we belong to:

- Nationality / ethnicity
- Family
- Place of education
- Religious affiliation
- Hobby / sport / interest groups
- Professional communities

Each group has its own culture, and we appropriate these into our individual culture (4:04--4:22). Mark asks students to list 5 identity groups they identify with strongly and rank them (4:22--4:32).

**Practical takeaway:** Just knowing there is a lens can help you look more objectively at encounters with different cultures (5:04--5:16).

## 2. Three Scenarios from the Lecture

Mark presents three scenarios demonstrating how cultural lenses produce different interpretations of identical events.

### Scenario 1: Eye Contact in an Interview (5:28--6:13)

| Western lens | Alternative cultural lens |
|---|---|
| Avoidance of direct eye contact = dishonesty or lack of confidence | Avoidance of direct eye contact = **sign of reverence / respect** |
| Making eye contact = engagement | Making direct eye contact = **disrespect** |

> "Without having some cultural sensitivity, you may have just said no to the right candidate for the job." (6:03)

### Scenario 2: Argument as Respect (6:15--7:22)

Mark recounts a personal experience presenting to a client. As a British person, he found open argument deeply uncomfortable.

| British lens (Mark's) | Client's cultural lens |
|---|---|
| Heated argument during presentation = they hated the proposal | Heated argument = **engagement, respect, treating you as an intelligent adversary** |
| "I was infuriated at the rudeness" | "My customer meant no offence" |
| "I had a miserable trip home" | They remained their preferred technology partner |

> "The same event has massively different meaning depending on the lens through which you interpret it." (6:58)

### Scenario 3: Left for Discussion

Mark deliberately leaves scenario 3 for the live group session: "Perhaps you get the idea. Now think through that one a little further and we'll discuss that together during the live session." (7:23--7:30)

## 3. Lynn M. Shore's Inclusion Framework

**Source:** Shore, L.M., Randel, A.E., Chung, B.G., Dean, M.A., Ehrhart, K.H. & Singh, G. (2011). "Inclusion and Diversity in Work Groups: A Review and Model for Future Research." *Journal of Management*, 37(4), 1262--1289.

Note: Mark refers to "Lynn M Shaw" in the transcript (7:48) --- the correct name is **Lynn M. Shore**. The framework draws on Brewer's **Optimal Distinctiveness Theory** (1991), which argues that people simultaneously need to feel they belong to a group AND that they are unique individuals.

### The 2x2 Matrix

Shore defines inclusion as: *"the degree to which an employee perceives that he or she is an esteemed member of the work group through experiencing treatment that satisfies his or her needs for belongingness and uniqueness."*

```
                        VALUE IN UNIQUENESS
                     Low                    High
                +------------------+------------------+
           High |   ASSIMILATION   |    INCLUSION     |
   BELONGING-   |                  |                  |
   NESS         | Treated as       | Treated as       |
                | insider, but     | insider AND      |
                | must conform to  | valued for what  |
                | group norms.     | makes you        |
                | "Treating        | different.       |
                | everyone the     |                  |
                | same."           | THE GOAL         |
                +------------------+------------------+
           Low  |   EXCLUSION      |  DIFFERENTIATION |
                |                  |                  |
                | Not part of the  | Valued for       |
                | in-group. Your   | unique skills,   |
                | differences are  | but not treated  |
                | not valued.      | as a business    |
                |                  | insider. Remain  |
                | "Employee will   | part of the      |
                | find it          | out-group.       |
                | difficult, if    |                  |
                | not impossible,  |                  |
                | to flourish."    |                  |
                +------------------+------------------+
```

### Mark's Commentary on Each Quadrant

**Exclusion** (Low belonging, Low uniqueness) --- 8:13--8:42
- Not treated as part of the in-group
- Differences and uniqueness not recognised as important or valuable
- Employee cannot flourish; organisation underperforms

**Assimilation** (High belonging, Low uniqueness) --- 8:42--9:38
- The most common response by organisations trying to improve
- Initially tolerate uniqueness, allow the person into the in-group
- Over time, expect conformity to cultural norms
- "Often wrapped up in the idea of **treating everyone equally**" (9:06)
- Mark's teaching experience: "Treating all students the same, although it sounded fair, turned out to be a terrible idea." (9:24--9:29)
- **Key insight: "In many circumstances, treating everyone the same is clearly unfair."** (19:28--19:35, reinforced)

**Differentiation** (Low belonging, High uniqueness) --- 9:43--10:05
- Uniqueness valued for the skills they bring
- But belongingness is missing --- still part of the out-group
- Not treated as business insiders

**Inclusion** (High belonging, High uniqueness) --- 10:06--10:16
- Treated as part of the group
- Not assimilated --- can remain an individual
- Both axes are high simultaneously

### Connection to Cultural Competence

> "If you don't make a conscious effort to understand individuals and what makes them different, then it's impossible to start making the right adjustments to allow them to bring both value to the team and to have a high sense of belonging." (10:19--10:33)

> "Typically, when we lack the knowledge to adapt to the way people would like to be treated, we revert to just being fair and being as nice to everybody as we can be. The problem is, this approach just doesn't work reliably across cultures." (10:33--10:46)

## 4. Three Cultural Models

Mark introduces three models for understanding cultural differences, treating them with deliberate caution: "as with any models, such as the ones we're using in engineering, they have their own limitations and risks" (11:16).

### 4.1 Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions (1960s--2010)

| Detail | Value |
|---|---|
| **Researcher** | Geert Hofstede (Dutch social psychologist, originally a mechanical engineer) |
| **Data source** | IBM --- founded their personnel research department |
| **Sample** | 117,000 IBM employees across 40+ countries |
| **Period** | 1967--1973 (data collection), 10+ years to publish |
| **Dimensions** | Originally 4, expanded to **6** |

#### The 6 Dimensions

| # | Dimension | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Power Distance (PDI)** | Egalitarian; subordinates expect to be consulted; flat hierarchies | Hierarchical; power centralised; subordinates expect to be told |
| 2 | **Individualism vs. Collectivism (IDV)** | Collectivist: loyalty to in-group, group harmony, "we" | Individualist: personal achievement, loose social ties, "I" |
| 3 | **Masculinity vs. Femininity (MAS)** | Feminine: cooperation, modesty, quality of life, caring | Masculine: competition, assertiveness, material success, heroism |
| 4 | **Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI)** | Comfortable with ambiguity, fewer rules, risk-tolerant | Need for certainty, formal rules, emotional resistance to change |
| 5 | **Long-term vs. Short-term Orientation (LTO)** | Short-term: tradition, social obligations, "saving face" | Long-term: pragmatism, thrift, perseverance, adapting traditions |
| 6 | **Indulgence vs. Restraint (IVR)** | Restraint: strict social norms suppress gratification | Indulgence: free gratification of desires, leisure, enjoyment |

*Dimensions 1--4 from original IBM study. Dimension 5 added by Bond (1991) from Chinese Values Survey of students in 23 countries. Dimension 6 added by Minkov (2010) from World Values Survey data across 93 countries.*

### 4.2 Trompenaars' Model of National Culture Differences (1997)

| Detail | Value |
|---|---|
| **Researcher** | Fons Trompenaars (and Charles Hampden-Turner) |
| **Data source** | Shell (and other multinationals including Philips, Heineken, Mars, Motorola, GM, Nike) |
| **Sample** | 8,500 managers across 43 countries (Mark's figure; later research expanded to 46,000+ managers in 40 countries) |
| **Published** | 1997, *Riding the Waves of Culture* |
| **Dimensions** | **7** (one more than Hofstede's original 6) |

#### The 7 Dimensions

| # | Dimension | Description | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Universalism vs. Particularism** | Rules apply equally to all vs. relationships and context determine application | Relationships |
| 2 | **Individualism vs. Communitarianism** | Self as individual vs. self as part of group | Relationships |
| 3 | **Neutral vs. Emotional (Affective)** | Emotions controlled and not expressed vs. emotions openly displayed | Relationships |
| 4 | **Specific vs. Diffuse** | Large public space / small private space vs. similar-sized public and private spaces; separation of work and personal life vs. overlap | Relationships |
| 5 | **Achievement vs. Ascription** | Status earned through accomplishments vs. status given by birth, age, gender, connections | Relationships |
| 6 | **Sequential vs. Synchronic Time** | Linear time, one thing at a time, strict schedules vs. multiple things simultaneously, past-present-future interrelated | Time |
| 7 | **Internal vs. External Direction** | Control environment ("nature is to be conquered") vs. adapt to environment ("go with the flow") | Environment |

*Dimensions 1--5 concern human relationships. Dimensions 6--7 concern time orientation and relationship with the environment.*

### 4.3 Erin Meyer's Culture Map (2014)

| Detail | Value |
|---|---|
| **Researcher** | Erin Meyer (American author and professor) |
| **Affiliation** | INSEAD Business School, France |
| **Data source** | Interviews, consulting experience, case studies (anecdotal, not large-scale survey) |
| **Published** | 2014, *The Culture Map: Breaking Through the Invisible Boundaries of Global Business* |
| **Dimensions** | **8** |

#### The 8 Scales

| # | Scale | Low end | High end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Communicating** | Low-context: precise, simple, explicit | High-context: nuanced, layered, read between lines |
| 2 | **Evaluating** | Direct negative feedback: frank, blunt | Indirect negative feedback: soft, diplomatic, wrapped in positives |
| 3 | **Persuading** | Principles-first (deductive): theory then facts | Applications-first (inductive): facts then conclusions |
| 4 | **Leading** | Egalitarian: flat, first-name basis | Hierarchical: deference, titles, status |
| 5 | **Deciding** | Consensual: group agreement before action | Top-down: decisions made by individuals with authority |
| 6 | **Trusting** | Task-based: trust built through work competence | Relationship-based: trust built through personal connection |
| 7 | **Disagreeing** | Confrontational: open debate, disagreement productive | Avoids confrontation: disagreement is threatening |
| 8 | **Scheduling** | Linear-time: sequential, deadline-focused | Flexible-time: adaptable, fluid, interruptions tolerated |

*Meyer's innovation: she maps cultures RELATIVE to each other rather than on absolute scales, making it practical for comparing specific country pairs.*

### Comparison Table

| Feature | Hofstede | Trompenaars | Meyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year** | 1967--1980 (expanded 2010) | 1997 | 2014 |
| **Dimensions** | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| **Data** | 117,000 IBM employees | 8,500+ Shell managers | Anecdotal / consulting |
| **Method** | Large-scale survey, factor analysis | Large-scale survey | Interviews, case studies |
| **Scientific rigour** | High (peer-reviewed, replicated) | Medium (survey-based) | Low (narrative, not scientific study) |
| **Criticism (from Mark)** | Can't reduce culture to 6 dimensions | Same oversimplification critique | "More anecdotal and not supported by any wide scale scientific study" |

## 5. Key Criticisms from Mark Graham

Mark devotes significant time (12:25--13:45) to warning about the dangers of these models. These are crucial points:

### "A little knowledge can be a dangerous thing" (12:51)

> "As you start to know some basic facts about other cultures, you start to take large leaps in assumption, which can be worse than actually remaining ignorant in the first place." (12:56--13:10)

### The individual trumps the group

> **"In each case, the individual's identity is more relevant than the culture of any group that they might belong to."** (12:45)

### Models don't account for mixed backgrounds

> "The model doesn't account for peoples who have a mixture of cultural backgrounds or people who have relocated." (13:19--13:26)

### National-level only

> "The models only look at groups of people on a national level, whereas ... we are the products of cultures of multiple groups that we associate with throughout our lives." (13:33--13:45)

### The engineering analogy (14:27--15:01)

Mark compares cultural models to engineering beam theory:
- First-year beam theory is a "highly simplified 1-dimensional representation of what really happens in nature" (14:33--14:40)
- We find it "very convenient for solving some problems" (14:40--14:44)
- In some situations it is "not detailed enough to be useful and it is possibly even risky" (14:44--14:50)
- **"The skill of the engineer is in knowing the strengths and limitations of the models and choosing how and when to apply them."** (14:54--15:01)

### Erin Meyer's own caveat

> "Erin Mayer alludes to this in her own work when she identifies that although stereotypes are sometimes wrong, they are often incomplete." (13:10--13:19)

## 6. Bennett's Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity (DMIS)

**Source:** Bennett, M.J. (1986). "A Developmental Approach to Training for Intercultural Sensitivity." *International Journal of Intercultural Relations*, 10(2), 179--196.

Bennett developed this model in 1986 based on constructivist perception theory and 15 years of experience training students about culture (17:06--17:15). It represents a **development continuum** --- not a checklist but a progression of increasing sophistication in how a person experiences cultural difference.

### The 6-Stage Continuum

```
ETHNOCENTRIC STAGES                     ETHNORELATIVE STAGES
(own culture = centre of reality)       (own culture = one of many valid worldviews)

+----------+    +------------+    +--------------+    +------------+    +------------+    +-------------+
|          |    |            |    |              |    |            |    |            |    |             |
|  DENIAL  |--->| DEFENCE /  |--->| MINIMISATION |--->| ACCEPTANCE |--->| ADAPTATION |--->| INTEGRATION |
|          |    | POLARISA-  |    |              |    |            |    |            |    |             |
|          |    | TION       |    |              |    |            |    |            |    |             |
+----------+    +------------+    +--------------+    +------------+    +------------+    +-------------+
                                        ^
                                        |
                               "Most people never
                                make it past the
                                 halfway point"
                                    (17:20)
```

### Stage-by-Stage Breakdown

#### Stage 1: DENIAL (17:26--18:05)

- Difference is noticed but no further thought given beyond the most obvious observable features
- May withdraw from the situation entirely
- In extreme cases, other cultures labelled as "subhuman" (colonial era example)
- "There is generally little thought and meaning has not been made of the differences"
- **Characterised by:** ignorance, isolation, disinterest

#### Stage 2: POLARISATION / DEFENCE (18:06--18:38)

- Some thought is happening; meaning is being attached to differences
- However, meaning is often **negative** --- "just because it is new and not understood"
- **Negative stereotyping** --- those behaviours attached to every person from that group
- "The automatic tendency will be to assume that one's own culture is superior"
- **"This is an us and them approach."** (18:34)
- **Characterised by:** stereotyping, superiority, demonisation of the "other"

#### Stage 3: MINIMISATION (18:39--19:35) --- THE HALFWAY WALL

- Pretending differences don't really exist: "we really are all the same anyway"
- Cultural differences made out to be inconsequential
- Assumes most human behaviour is innate (born with it)
- **"Possibly the most subversive, as it might have the pretence of being inclusive, but it is still seeing the world from a monocultural perspective."** (18:58--19:08)
- Example: the West projecting democracy as "the only natural and obvious outcome for any developed nation" --- "a seriously monocultural viewpoint" (19:08--19:20)
- Connected to Shore's Assimilation quadrant: "we'll just treat everybody the same because that is fair"
- **"In many circumstances, treating everyone the same is clearly unfair."** (19:28--19:35)
- **Characterised by:** false universalism, colourblindness, "I don't see difference"

> Mark's critical observation: **"Many, if not most people never make it past the halfway point."** (17:20--17:28)

#### Stage 4: ACCEPTANCE (19:35--19:56)

- Showing signs of empathy for how others think and behave
- "We accept that they do things as they do"
- Differences seen as "an essential part of life and an opportunity for curiosity and not value judgement"
- **Acceptance does not mean agreement** --- details may not be deeply understood yet
- **Characterised by:** curiosity, respect, non-judgemental observation

#### Stage 5: ADAPTATION (19:57--20:27)

- Increased intercultural sensitivity
- Begin to **change behaviour and actions** to work together more effectively
- Requires a "working knowledge of the other culture"
- Must understand some of the reasons **why** things are different
- "This evaluation is something that takes time and practise" (20:16)
- Requires continuous attention as cultures change, even within the same group (20:19--20:27)
- **Characterised by:** behavioural flexibility, frame-shifting, empathy in action

#### Stage 6: INTEGRATION (implied, 20:27+)

- "The more you learn, the more you realise you don't know" (20:27--20:32)
- Does not happen automatically; requires **intentionality** (20:32--20:37)
- Living in another culture alone will NOT move you along the continuum (20:37--20:50)
- "Plenty of examples of groups that have lived in close proximity to each other for decades that still have no understanding and no empathy for each other" (20:37--20:50)
- **Characterised by:** constructive marginality, identity not defined by any single culture, effortless code-switching

### Bennett's Quotation (shown on slide, 15:40--16:02)

Mark references a quotation on screen (slides not available). The paraphrased message is:

> It is not so much about how much you **know** about a culture, but an awareness of the **processes** that are happening when we work across cultures that is important. (16:04--16:15)

And the complementary insight:

> You never really **become** culturally competent. Your learning is a continuous process with a tendency for each new experience to generate a new set of questions rather than all the answers being completed. (16:15--16:32)

## 7. Practical Advice (21:39--22:18)

Mark closes with four concrete steps when encountering a cultural difference:

1. **Pause** --- "take a pause and try to avoid interpreting the situation too quickly" (21:43--21:50)
2. **Think** --- "take some time to think through what is happening" (21:50--21:53)
3. **Consider your own lens** --- "think about your own experience and culture and consider how this might be causing you to see what you believe" (21:53--22:01)
4. **Take their perspective** --- "try to put yourself in the other person's perspective and see whether that might influence how they have interpreted the situation" (22:01--22:09)
5. **Learn more** --- "If you find that your understanding of their culture is lacking, that's a really useful insight to come to. Why not try to find out a bit more?" (22:09--22:18)

## 9. D7 Application --- Apollo's Reflection

### Your Context

Ukrainian robotics student on a multicultural Bristol MSc team (5 members). You bring a specific cultural lens shaped by Ukrainian engineering culture, the war context, and being one of relatively few Eastern European students on the programme.

### Cultural Lens: "One Person Delivers"

Ukrainian engineering culture (especially post-Soviet technical education) often emphasises individual technical mastery --- one person takes ownership and delivers. This contrasts with the British collaborative/consensus approach and the diverse team dynamics of your MSc group. Recognising this as a cultural lens (not "the right way") is itself a move from Minimisation toward Acceptance on Bennett's continuum.

### Applying the Models

**Shore's Inclusion Framework:**
- Reflect on whether your team operated in the Assimilation quadrant ("let's all just work the same way") or achieved genuine Inclusion (valuing each member's unique strengths while maintaining belonging).
- Did you adapt your communication to teammates' preferences, or default to "treating everyone the same"?
- Mark's line: "treating everyone the same is clearly unfair" --- this directly applies to adapting your leadership/communication style per teammate.

**Hofstede:**
- Power Distance: Ukrainian culture scores higher on PDI than the UK. How did this affect your expectations of hierarchy vs. the flat British team structure?
- Uncertainty Avoidance: Ukraine scores high on UAI. Did this manifest as wanting detailed plans while British teammates were comfortable with ambiguity?
- Individualism: Both UK and Ukraine score relatively high on individualism, but the expressions differ.

**Bennett's DMIS:**
- Where were you at the START of the project? (Likely Minimisation --- "we're all engineers, the technical work is what matters")
- Where did you move TO? (Acceptance or early Adaptation --- recognising that teammates' different approaches were valid, not inferior)
- Evidence of Adaptation: changing your communication style, adjusting expectations, finding ways to leverage rather than suppress difference.

### Hatton Level 4 (Critical Reflection) Moves

To reach L4, you need to show awareness of **how your own cultural assumptions shaped your interpretation of events**. Use this structure:

1. **Describe** a team interaction that caused friction or surprise
2. **Identify your cultural lens**: "As someone from a Ukrainian engineering background, I initially interpreted this as..."
3. **Reframe using theory**: "Graham's concept of 'we see what we believe' (2026) suggests I was attaching meaning based on my own cultural programming, not responding to the actual event."
4. **Apply a model**: "Using Bennett's DMIS, I recognise this reaction as [Minimisation/Defence]. Moving toward Acceptance required me to..."
5. **Show growth**: "By the end of the project, I had adapted my approach by..."

### Example Paragraph (D7 style)

> Working in a multicultural MSc team exposed assumptions I had not previously examined. As Graham (2026) argues, "we see what we believe" --- our personal culture acts as an invisible lens. My Ukrainian engineering background instilled a value of individual ownership: one person takes responsibility and delivers. When a teammate's contribution style differed from this expectation, my initial reaction sat at what Bennett (1986) terms the Minimisation stage --- I assumed "we're all engineers, so we should all work the same way." Graham's inclusion framework, drawing on Shore et al. (2011), reveals this as Assimilation rather than true Inclusion: high belonging but low value of uniqueness. Recognising that "treating everyone the same is clearly unfair" (Graham, 2026) prompted me to adapt my communication approach to each teammate's strengths, moving toward Bennett's Acceptance stage.
