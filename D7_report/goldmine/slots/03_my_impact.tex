\subsection{3a. How my contributions drove the team forward}

The honest thesis of this section: the simulation framework I built is the reason this project produced verifiable engineering output despite having faulty hardware for most of the project timeline. I want to defend that claim with mechanism, not assertion, because at Merit-level it reads as self-flattery and at Distinction-level it requires evidence.

\textbf{The CV pipeline: detection to GPS estimation.} My formal CV responsibility expanded into something far more complex than training a model. I built the full detection-to-GPS pipeline: take a camera frame, run YOLOv8 inference, convert the pixel coordinates of a detection into a GPS position estimate for the dummy on the ground. That conversion is not trivial. The drone's pitch and roll affect where the camera is pointing, so I had to correct for attitude. Altitude affects the scale of the image, so I had to compensate for that. And a single detection gives a GPS estimate with significant error --- several metres off, sometimes more --- so I developed and tested multiple averaging techniques: inverse-variance weighting, spatial clustering, Kalman filtering, each with different trade-offs in accuracy versus latency.

The constraint was data. We had one real flight video, provided by Sid. One video. I tested every averaging technique against that single recording. I deduced ground truth from the video itself --- working backwards from known landmarks to establish where the dummy actually was --- and benchmarked each technique against that ground truth. I also used the video to retrain the model: 16 labelled frames extracted at native resolution, combined with 300 synthetic images and 50 negatives. The retrained model (mAP50 = 0.995 on validation) was a drop-in replacement. I squeezed everything I could from extremely limited data.

The pipeline drove the team forward in a specific way: Robin's state machine needed a GPS position for the detected target. Without a reliable estimate, his flight logic had nothing to navigate towards. The interface I provided --- captured images plus estimated GPS coordinates, corrected for attitude and altitude --- was what Robin consumed. Building that interface was ``quite expensive,'' as I described it at the time. But it meant Robin could develop his flight script against a defined contract without understanding the CV internals.

I also did thorough camera calibration in the lab --- field of view and focal length measurements using a tape measure at known distances, validated against the DJI video using a click-to-calibrate tool I built. I created post-processing scripts for the DJI footage, calibration scripts for camera parameters, and video analysis tools --- all designed to maximise learning from that single real flight recording.

\textbf{The simulation framework --- a Kolb cycle.} This was the contribution that consumed the most hours and had the most ambiguous team impact. I want to walk through a full Kolb (1984) cycle, because I used it deliberately rather than retrospectively, and it is the piece of experiential learning that most changed my engineering practice during this project.

\textit{Concrete experience}: Two practice flight days where the team had everything prepared --- an in-depth simulation we wanted to test, a suite of test scripts we wanted to run --- and the hardware did not work. We were given faulty hardware. Things just did not work, and we were not sure what the issues were. All the other teams had working hardware from the start. Our hardware was not functional until Easter. The first two practice flight days effectively did not count. The whole team felt demotivated --- me included. I had loads of things I wanted to run and test.

\textit{Reflective observation}: The team's entire definition of ``progress'' had silently assumed hardware access we did not have. Robin could not test his state machine. Edward could not validate his interface. Many things could only be tested with real GPS signal, which was not available in the lab --- anything requiring GPS coordinates could only be verified on flight days. We were working on hypotheses about what might work, but could not actually test those hypotheses without flying. The only person who was not fully blocked was me, because I had a laptop with SITL. That asymmetry was the data point that mattered.

\textit{Abstract conceptualisation}: If the vision module exposed a single interface (\texttt{detect\_in\_image(frame)} returning \texttt{(found, x, y, conf)}) and auto-selected between the full model on laptop and the lightweight model on Pi, and if the config auto-detected its hardware, then the same state machine, geofence, and search pattern could run in both places. Anything that ran in simulation would, in principle, run on hardware. The key design decision was to make the vision module the ONLY file that touches CV. Everything else calls the same function. You can change the model, preprocessing, or backend without touching any other file. That modularity was not aesthetic; it was risk mitigation. In Graham's (2026) risk management framework, it reduced both the \textit{likelihood} of a platform-incompatibility failure (by abstracting the backend) and the \textit{consequence} (by isolating the blast radius to a single file).

\textit{Active experimentation}: Instead of just being frustrated, I decided to be proactive: go ahead and spend more time figuring things out in simulation, aiming for a solid final product. The simulation grew to 2508 lines with simulated GPS drift, camera shake, CV throttle, and a TFLite backend path. I ran it thousands of times, observing closely. I simulated camera blur. I thought through everything that could go wrong. Instead of just holding problems in my head, I had a concrete simulation to run and test against. This helped me anticipate issues ahead of time rather than discovering them during the real mission. Robin suggested the repulsive force concept for no-fly zone avoidance --- I thought it was a great idea, got into it, and developed it over several days of experimentation, testing edge cases like what happens when the dummy is placed very close to the no-fly zone boundary. The simulation could verify all R01--R12 requirements.

The Kolb cycle taught me something I had not previously articulated: simulation-first is not a personal preference. It is an engineering obligation when hardware access is unreliable. And the obligation has limits. The simulator could not predict real motion blur at 5\,m/s with a real IMX296 sensor. It could not find the descent stall we eventually accepted as a SITL artefact. It could not tell us what GPS timing lag (100--200\,ms latency, causing approximately 1\,m error at 5\,m/s) would do to a multi-pass target lock --- that insight came from DJI video analysis, after the fact. The simulation's value is bounded by the fidelity of its models, and I was honest about that only because I had been forced to be.

I spent 3--4$\times$ more hours than expected on this project, largely because of the simulation work. I communicated the simulation to the team and they were happy with it. I believe the simulation was almost ready to run in real mode, but to be confident about it we would have needed more flight days.

\textbf{The progressive test ladder --- risk assessment.} I wrote progressive test scripts for empirical drone performance testing --- blur effect at different speeds, detection range versus altitude, detection rate at various conditions. These formed a safety-critical test ladder designed for step-by-step confidence building before attempting a full mission:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Bench commands} --- test individual MAVLink commands on the bench, no propellers. Reduces the \textit{likelihood} of a command-path failure reaching the field.
\item \textbf{Fly GPS waypoints, no CV} --- the drone flies a simple pattern under script control with no perception involved. Isolates navigation risk from perception risk. The \textit{consequence} of a CV bug is zero because CV is not running.
\item \textbf{Manual flight, passive detection} --- pilot flies on RC, Pi runs camera and AI, logs detections, buzzer beeps, sends ZERO commands. Tests perception in real conditions with zero \textit{consequence}: the pilot has full control, the system observes only.
\item \textbf{Autonomous search, logging only} --- the full pipeline runs but detection triggers logging, not action. The \textit{consequence} of a false positive is a log entry, not an unwanted descent.
\item \textbf{Full autonomous mission} --- only attempted after four prior steps have passed.
\end{enumerate}

In Graham's (2026) risk assessment framework, each step reduces either the likelihood or the consequence of a failure mode, and no step introduces both navigation risk and perception risk simultaneously until step 5. The design is sound. The execution was not: due to limited flight time, we never ran the full ladder on real hardware. The residual risk of an unexecuted mitigation is the same as the residual risk of no mitigation at all.

\textbf{Dashboards and monitoring tools.} I built a passive watch dashboard for monitoring and detection during manual flight, and a diagnostics dashboard showing all drone subsystems at once with the ability to switch between different vision models live to compare performance. Both were built for the Pi (headless, browser-based) so they could run on actual drone hardware without a screen attached. These tools were designed for the pilot and ground operator, not for me. \textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: Did anyone other than you actually use these dashboards on flight day? \textbf{Going with YOLO person detection.} Edward did train a CV model as I had delegated. We did not end up using it. We went with a standard YOLO person detection model instead --- the safe choice. The reasons: not enough flight days to collect realistic aerial training data, and we could not run proper experiments to validate a custom model. We did have trained models for detecting different items of interest, but in the end the YOLO person detection was safer given our limited real-world testing. In Graham's risk framework, this reduced the \textit{likelihood} of a detection failure on demo day at the cost of reduced specificity. The trade-off was correct given the constraints.

\textbf{The flight day outcome.} Everything came together on the actual flight day without prior real-hardware testing of the full pipeline. This worked because the simulation was thorough --- but it was also somewhat lucky. The fact that it all came together on demo day without any real testing beforehand says something about the quality of the simulation work. Building on the success of the Easter days (about one and a half functioning flight days before the performance), the team managed to deliver a minimum viable product. It was not the standard we wanted --- on the very first flight day we had already had a bunch of things ready to run. But we did the best we could given the hardware limitations, and what we delivered was solid given the constraints.

\textbf{Evaluating my own effectiveness (M16.d).} Against the criterion of ``did my contributions unblock teammates or only advance my own work,'' the judgement is mixed. The CV pipeline was genuinely team-enabling: Robin's state machine consumed it directly. The simulation framework kept the project moving when hardware was unavailable. The handoff documentation worked: Robin integrated without clarifying questions. But the simulator itself (2508 lines) was edited by zero teammates across the entire project. The unit tests I wrote (127 functions across 6 files) were run by zero teammates. The cause is not that these artefacts were bad --- it is that I built them as solo infrastructure rather than collaborative tools. I was effective at producing artefacts but ineffective at producing shared ownership, and those are different kinds of effectiveness.

\textbf{Evaluating my team's effectiveness (M16.d-team).} We did the best we could with what we had. We could verify all 12 requirements in simulation and demonstrated a minimum viable product on real hardware. The gap was caused by hardware unavailability (external, not our fault), coordination gaps (internal, our responsibility), and insufficient investment in team processes (internal, largely my responsibility as the person who set the technical pace). Our Tuckman progression --- brief Forming, extended frustration, late integration, compressed performance --- reflects a team that invested too little time in managing itself, exactly the pattern Graham warns about. We treated process as overhead rather than infrastructure.

\textbf{Communication methods evaluated (M17.d).} I used several communication methods across the project:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Technical handoff documentation} (Robin) --- pipeline docs for the CV-to-state-machine interface, with exact CLI commands, JSON schema, parsing examples
\item \textbf{Browser-based ground station} (the pilot and team) --- headless web dashboard accessible from any device at the field
\item \textbf{Complaint letter} (Steve, the course organiser --- a non-engineering audience) --- formal, factual, deliberately restrained, signed by all five members
\item \textbf{Field reference card} --- copy-paste-ready terminal commands for flight day with no internet access
\item \textbf{Progressive test scripts} (team) --- numbered scripts that enforce a step-by-step confidence-building process
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{3b. What I would add or re-focus on in future projects}

The specific gap I want to name: I mistook technical leadership for team leadership. I now see they are orthogonal --- you can build great tools that do not enable anyone --- and in this project the gap showed up as a simple fact: the core simulation and mission files were edited by zero teammates. That is not an ownership success; it is an ownership failure. I owned too much, shared too little, and called it diligence.

Graham's (2026) risk management lecture introduces three pillars of obligation: moral, economic, and legal. Applying the economic lens: the team's ``currency'' is shared capability, and a massive codebase that only one person can edit is an illiquid asset. Code that no one else can maintain is not a team asset; it is a personal portfolio piece that happens to live in a shared repository.

Three mechanisms for future refocusing:

\textbf{1. Build for named users from the start.} Before starting any new tool, I will name at least one teammate who will use it and document their name in the commit message. The test I should have been running: if I had built the simulator explicitly for Robin to test his flight logic without a drone, would I have designed it differently? Yes --- I would have made the keyboard controls match his flight-script commands, not mine. The tool would have been less elegant and more useful. A tool built for ``the team'' is often a tool built for nobody.

\textbf{2. Execute the test ladder, not just design it.} I am proud of the progressive test ladder as a design. I am not proud that we never ran it. Next time, I would build the test ladder AND build the project schedule around it --- not as an optional extra, but as the critical path. In Graham's risk framework, an unexecuted mitigation carries the same residual risk as no mitigation at all. The would-have-liked-to is not a plan.

\textbf{3. Earlier integration, not just late collaboration.} Robin and I worked well together once integration was needed, and it went well. But we started integrating late --- after Easter, under time pressure. If we had integrated earlier, even with stub data, we would have found interface mismatches sooner and built shared understanding of the codebase earlier. Integration is not a phase; it is a practice.

Applying Graham's risk framework to these mechanisms: Mechanism 1 reduces the \textit{likelihood} of building unused tools. Mechanism 2 reduces the \textit{consequence} of hardware surprises (the test ladder becomes the critical path, not a supplement). Mechanism 3 reduces both --- early integration transfers knowledge (likelihood) and creates backup capability (consequence). The residual risk: even with these mechanisms, I may still default to solo work under time pressure. That residual risk is not zero, and I acknowledge it rather than pretending the mechanisms are sufficient.

Applying Graham's ALARP principle: is my current approach to team contribution ``as low as reasonably practicable'' in terms of team risk? No. If I had been ill during the last two weeks of the project, the team would have lost access to the CV pipeline, the simulation framework, the ground station, the test ladder, and all monitoring tools. That bus factor of one is intolerable. The mitigations I have proposed reduce the risk to tolerable. But they require me to accept slower initial progress in exchange for faster recovery from disruption, and that trade-off is the one I find hardest to make.
