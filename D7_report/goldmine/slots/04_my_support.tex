\subsection{4b. What I did that helped others work more effectively}

I will put specific episodes on record, because ``I gave constructive feedback when needed'' is the single most common Merit-ceiling sentence in UK engineering reflection, and I'm not going to write it.

\textbf{Episode 1: Building Robin's integration interface.}

After the hardware issues were sorted, Robin needed detection output for his state machine. I provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. This interface was not trivial --- it required attitude correction for pitch and roll, altitude compensation, and dealing with significant single-estimate error. I built it specifically so Robin's state machine could consume vision data without touching my CV code.

The result: Robin integrated without asking me a single clarifying question for two days. I had communicated the simulation work to the team and they were happy with it. The interface worked because I'd anticipated what Robin would need rather than just exposing whatever internal data structure I happened to use.

The reflection: building that interface was the highest-leverage work I did on the project, measured by teammate-hours-unblocked per author-hour-invested. I had previously treated integration work as a cost paid for politeness. In fact it was more impactful than most of the solo features I built. That reframing --- from ``overhead'' to ``highest leverage'' --- is one of the core belief changes I take from this project.

\textbf{Episode 2: The complaint letter on behalf of the team.}

The team had been provided with faulty hardware. All other teams had working hardware from the start. Our hardware wasn't functional until Easter. The first two practice days effectively didn't count, and Easter shouldn't really count as a work day since we sacrificed holiday time. Rather than letting frustration dissipate as corridor complaints, I channelled it into a formal written artefact --- a complaint letter to the course organiser documenting what had happened and its impact on the team's ability to meet learning outcomes.

I led this communication because I had the clearest view of what the hardware failures had cost in engineering terms. The letter's tone was deliberately measured and factual --- documenting what happened, not assigning personal blame.

\textbf{Episode 3: Tools and infrastructure for the team.}

The quieter half of my support work was building infrastructure the team could use without my presence:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Progressive test scripts --- numbered and ordered so nobody could skip a step, designed for step-by-step confidence building before attempting anything risky
\item Browser-based dashboards and monitoring tools --- headless-capable, so anyone with a browser could see what the drone was doing without needing a screen attached to the drone
\item A field reference card for no-internet flight day use --- written after the first cancelled day when I realised we'd be standing in a field with no internet
\item Camera calibration data --- FOV and focal length measurements done in the lab so the GPS estimation pipeline would have real numbers
\item Video analysis tools --- built to maximise learning from the single DJI flight video
\item The simulation framework itself --- which served as the team's backup when hardware was unavailable, and which could verify all R01--R12 requirements
\end{itemize}

These artefacts were not glamorous. They didn't train models or fly drones. But they were the substrate that let the project produce results when hardware didn't cooperate.

\subsection{What I leave believing}

I came to this project as someone whose reflex under failure was ``one person delivers.'' I leave believing its near-inversion: the engineer who delivers alone is one step away from being the single point of failure in whatever they build.

The close collaboration with Robin during integration, and the fact that the system worked on flight day, told me that shared interfaces are more valuable than solo features. The simulation kept the project alive when hardware failed --- and I'm proud of that. But the simulation was also something only I could maintain, and that fragility is the lesson I carry forward.

The team did a great job given the circumstances. We did the best we could with the hardware we were provided. Our limitations were not of our own making --- we were given non-functional hardware and facilities that constrained what we could test. Despite all of this, we produced a working system that demonstrated on demo day. Building on the success of that one and a half functioning days before the performance, the team came up with something decent for the demo --- a minimum viable product. Not the standard we wanted; on the very first flight day we'd already had much more ambitious things ready to run. But we did the best we could, and what we delivered was solid given the constraints.

Applying Tuckman's model to the full arc: we Formed briefly at the first meeting, struggled through a long period of frustration as hardware failed, reached a fragile Norming during Easter when things finally started working, and compressed our Performing into the demo day. We are now in Tuckman and Jensen's (1977) Adjourning stage. I feel the tension they describe --- not because the project was a resounding success, but because I can see the team we could have been if we'd invested in trust-building from the start instead of trust-avoidance through code.

What remains unresolved: I don't yet know how to build trust quickly in a team of strangers. The mechanisms I've proposed --- pair-programming budget, named-beneficiary test, documentation paired with conversation --- are process scaffolds, not relationship tools. The relationship part, the part Lencioni says is the foundation of everything, I still find uncomfortable, and I suspect I will continue to find it uncomfortable. But I know, now, that the discomfort is data, not a deficiency, and that ignoring it is more expensive than sitting with it. That is the lesson I will carry.
