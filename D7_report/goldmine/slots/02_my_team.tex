\subsection{2a. Team structure --- how it formed and what changed}

There were five of us: Apollo, Robin, Harish, Edward, and Zian. After the first team meeting we looked at all the tasks the project needed and split them up. Harish took hardware setup and communications between components. Zian took path planning. Robin took the flight state machine. Apollo and Edward shared computer vision --- I delegated the CV model training to Edward while I focused on the detection-to-GPS pipeline, simulation, and integration.

\textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: Was this structure discussed explicitly in the meeting, or did it just happen? Did someone suggest the split, or did people volunteer? Was there any disagreement about who took what --- or did everyone just grab what felt natural?]}

We deliberately did not name a single technical lead. The brief (R08) devolves autonomy-level choices to the team and we wanted distributed ownership. This felt sensible at the time.

Mapping this against Tuckman's (1965) model, our Forming stage was brief --- arguably too brief. Graham warns in Professional Practice that teams which spend ``too little time forming, which means no building of trust in the team, no discussion of its aims or its goals, and just jumping straight into the storming phase'' produce the worst-case scenario where the team eventually needs to reform under pressure. That is approximately what happened to us. We split the work and started building. We did not spend time discussing how we would communicate, how we would handle disagreements, or what ``done'' meant for each module.

The structure then drifted --- silently. The team had a while to work on everything before the flight days came. We did extensive preparatory work, but when it came to flight days we got quite disheartened and demotivated. The core issue was that we did not know what actually worked and what did not until we actually flew the drone. Many things could only be tested with real GPS signal, which was not available in the lab --- anything requiring GPS coordinates could only be verified on flight days. We were working on hypotheses about what might work, but could not test those hypotheses without flying.

As practice flight days failed and hardware remained non-functional, the project's centre of gravity migrated from the test field to my laptop --- the only surface where forward progress was possible. I was running SITL simulation, writing test scripts, building the web ground station and dashboards, while Harish waited for working hardware and Robin waited for flight data to test his state machine against. Nobody declared this shift; it just accumulated. My role expanded from ``CV specialist'' to something much broader without anyone consciously choosing that.

In Lencioni's (2002) terms, this silent drift is a textbook expression of Dysfunction~4, avoidance of accountability: without commitment to a clear plan of who owns what \textit{this week}, there is nothing to measure against, and low standards prevail. We had committed to a structure in the first meeting, but we had not committed to a mechanism for detecting when that structure no longer matched reality.

\textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: Was there ever a moment where someone said ``wait, you're doing too much'' or ``we need to re-split''? Or did the drift just go unchallenged? Did you ever raise it yourself?]}

The structure changed most visibly after Easter, when the hardware finally started working. Robin needed detection output for his state machine, and we entered a period of close coordination. I provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. Building this interface was expensive --- it required attitude correction for pitch and roll, altitude compensation, and dealing with significant single-estimate error. Ideas were discussed between us as colleagues. This was the point where the team actually started functioning as an integrated unit rather than parallel streams, and it happened late --- not by design, but because hardware failures had kept us apart.

One reading of this is that our team structure failed. Another is that it succeeded in the way that matters: when integration was finally possible, the interface between Robin's state machine and my CV output worked. Robin consumed my output without needing me beside him. I conclude the structure worked not because it was well-designed at the start, but because we had the capacity to adapt when circumstances forced it. That capacity, I now believe, is more valuable than getting the initial design right.

\ebi\ We should have revisited the structure formally at regular intervals, not only when external events forced it. A standing agenda item --- ``does our current structure still match the work?'' --- would have caught the drift before it became entrenched. The mechanism was simple; the discipline to use it was what we lacked.

\subsection{2b. Most impactful elements of team working}

The most impactful team-working element, measured by what it actually unblocked, was the close collaboration between Robin and me during the integration period after Easter.

Robin and I were the two proactive members of the team. After the hardware issues were sorted, Robin needed my CV output for his state machine. I provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. Robin suggested the repulsive force concept for no-fly zone avoidance --- I thought it was a great idea, got into it, and developed it over several days of experimentation, testing edge cases like what happens when the dummy is placed very close to the NFZ boundary. Ideas were discussed between us as colleagues; neither of us could have produced the result alone. This kind of collaborative ideation --- Robin proposes a concept, I implement and stress-test it, we iterate --- was the most productive mode we found.

What made this collaboration work was a clear interface contract: Robin's script handled flight decisions, my code handled perception and GPS estimation. We did not need to understand each other's internals. The hours I spent writing interface documentation bought Robin significant unblocked integration time --- he could wire my detection output into his mission script without asking clarifying questions. I had previously treated handoff docs as a cost paid for politeness. In fact they were the highest-leverage work I did on this project, measured by teammate-hours-unblocked per author-hour-invested.

\www\ The progressive test ladder was the second most impactful element. I organised all flight tests into numbered steps (\texttt{tests/flight/0a\_cube\_commands.py} through \texttt{4\_detect\_and\_center.py}), with a strict rule: never skip a step. Each step builds trust before adding risk. This was, in Graham's (2026) risk management framework, a mitigation that reduced both likelihood and consequence. Due to limited flight time, the full test suite was never run on real hardware --- but the ladder shaped how we approached testing. When we finally ran on the Pi during Easter, the delta between simulation and hardware turned out to be manageable: a few coordinate bugs and a corrupt calibration file. The system came up because the simulation and test infrastructure had caught everything else.

But I need to name the negative side too, because it was equally defining: \textbf{the hardware failures and their effect on team morale.}

The team had prepared thoroughly before the flight days came. I had an in-depth simulation, a progressive test ladder, multiple dashboards, test scripts for every subsystem. But when the practice flight days arrived, we were given faulty hardware. Things just did not work, and we were not sure what the issues were. The first two practice flight days were effectively lost. All the other teams had working hardware from the start; ours was not functional until Easter.

This was deeply demoralising. During those failed days, the whole team felt demotivated --- including me. I had loads of things I wanted to run and test. But instead of just being frustrated, I decided to be proactive: I would go ahead and spend more time figuring things out in simulation, aiming for a thorough final product. The simulation was very useful and very detailed --- I simulated camera blur, thought through everything that could go wrong, and ran the simulation many times observing closely. Instead of holding problems in my head, I had a concrete simulation to test against.

The frustration was compounded by organisational problems on flight days. The Wi-Fi signal was prioritised for teams that were actively flying, while the other teams were supposed to be tinkering on the ground. But without good Wi-Fi, we could not connect to our drone to test or configure anything. There were IP address and naming conflicts. The hardware that was not working was not our responsibility --- it was provided to us.

The turning point came during Easter, when the team sacrificed holiday time for two extra practice days. On the first there were still some issues, but the hardware problems were finally resolved. Getting confirmation that the state machine worked on real hardware was the moment the team's energy shifted from frustration to focus. From that point, we had roughly one and a half functioning flight days before the performance demo.

In Tuckman's terms, those failed flight days forced us through an accelerated Storming phase --- not a productive storming of ideas, but a collective crisis of confidence. The question was not ``whose approach is better'' but ``does anything we built actually work.'' That is a harder kind of storming because there is no resolution through debate; the only resolution is working hardware.

Everything came together on the actual demo day without prior real-hardware testing of the full pipeline. This worked because the simulation was thorough --- but it was also somewhat lucky. The fact that it all came together pretty well says something about the quality of the preparation, but I would have much preferred the step-by-step confidence-building approach I had designed.

Looking at this through Lencioni's framework, the hardware crisis actually pushed us past some dysfunctions. The shared adversity --- all five of us frustrated by the same external constraint --- created a kind of forced trust. We stopped worrying about who owned what module and started worrying about whether anything worked at all. That is not the healthy path to Tuckman's Norming stage, but it was the path we had.

The complaint letter we sent to the course organiser about the hardware situation was, in a sense, the team's first genuinely collective action --- all five names, one shared grievance, articulated in writing with measured factual tone. I led that communication because I had the clearest view of what the hardware failures had cost in engineering terms, and because I had the written evidence to back every claim. \textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: Did Robin or anyone else review the letter before it went out? Did anyone push back on the tone? Did Steve respond? Any detail here strengthens the conflict-management evidence --- this counts as external conflict management.]}

\ebi\ The flight day failures exposed a gap in our team's resilience planning. We had no contingency for ``what if the hardware does not work on the day.'' My simulation was a partial answer --- it let me keep working --- but it was not a team-level response. A team-level contingency would have been: ``if hardware fails on practice day~1, here is a prioritised list of what each person does with their ground time.'' We never wrote that list. Instead, most of the team waited for the hardware to work, and when it did not, they had nothing structured to do.

\subsection{2c. What I would change about team structure}

\textcolor{red}{[APOLLO: What would you genuinely change? Below are three changes inferred from your context. But what does your gut say? Maybe it is something simpler --- ``I would pick teammates differently'' or ``I would push back on the hardware earlier'' or ``I would delegate less to Edward and more to Robin'' or ``I would insist on weekly check-ins from day one.'' Your honest answer, even if blunt, will be stronger than anything I can infer.]}

Three concrete structural changes, each tied to a specific failure I observed.

\textbf{Change~1: Fortnightly structure review.} I would institute a standing 15-minute check-in every two weeks with one question: ``does our current task allocation still match the work that actually needs doing?'' The silent drift --- where I accumulated responsibilities nobody assigned me --- would have been caught by any regular review of who was doing what. The falsifiable signal: zero surprises at integration time about who owns which module. Graham warns that teams which ``don't bother to think about how they work, possibly because they hate talking about management, will have to spend even more time trying to manage the team.'' That was us exactly.

\textbf{Change~2: Front-loaded interface contracts.} I would front-load interface contracts \textit{before} parallel work begins --- a single early session on the data format between my CV output and Robin's state machine would have saved integration time later. When Robin finally needed my output, building the interface was ``quite expensive'' --- attitude correction, altitude compensation, averaging techniques for noisy single estimates. If we had agreed on the interface shape in the first weeks, I could have built to that contract from the start instead of retrofitting it.

Drawing on the down-selection tools from Graham's (2025) innovation lecture, I would also propose using a structured decision framework for architecture choices, with explicit weighted criteria rather than informal discussion. Graham's warning resonates: ``if you skip the requirements analysis, you'll most likely find that the solution selection takes much longer anyway as the team will find it harder to agree on what good is supposed to look like.''

\textbf{Change~3: A hardware failure contingency owned by the whole team.} This is the change most specific to our project. I would write, in the first week, a one-page document answering: ``if the hardware does not work on flight day, what does each person do with their time?'' My answer to that question was to build simulation --- but that was my personal response, not a team plan. Edward and Zian, who would do what they were assigned but were not self-directed, had no equivalent fallback when the hardware failed. A team-level contingency would have given everyone structured work during the dead flight days, instead of leaving people waiting while I kept building.

\ebi\ The deeper lesson, which connects to our Tuckman progression, is about how much time to invest in the team versus the technical work. Graham presents a model showing that good teams spend \textit{more} time managing themselves early and \textit{less} later, while poor teams do the opposite. We were the poor-team pattern: almost no time on team management early on, then managing the fallout of hardware failures and unclear ownership. If I could restart, I would advocate for one full session in the first week dedicated to nothing but team process: task allocation, interface contracts, communication cadence, a shared definition of ``done'' for each module, and a contingency plan for hardware failure. That session would have cost a few hours of technical work and saved days of frustration and rework.

Applying Lencioni's antidotes directly: I would start with a personal histories exercise in the first meeting --- sharing backgrounds, working styles, and what each person needs to do their best work. The point is not to become friends; it is to build enough trust that disagreements feel normal, not threatening. And I would institute what Lencioni calls ``publication of goals and standards'' --- a shared document listing what each person has committed to deliver, by when, with what quality bar. The reason I did not do this is that it felt bureaucratic and paternalistic. The reason I should have is that, as Lencioni puts it, ``peer pressure in the team, providing there's a foundation of trust, is way more effective than a bureaucratic measurement system.'' We had neither peer pressure nor a measurement system. We had me building things and hoping the team would notice.

The honest summary of 2c: I would change the team's investment in its own processes, and the person I would need to convince first is myself, because my instinct is always to write code rather than write a decision log, and every lesson in this section is a lesson about that instinct being wrong.
