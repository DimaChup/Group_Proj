\subsection{1a. Roles and responsibilities --- explicit, implicit, and how they shifted}

After the first team meeting we looked at every task the project needed and split them up explicitly. Harish took hardware setup and communications between components. Zian took path planning. Robin took the flight state machine. Edward and I shared computer vision --- I delegated the CV model training to Edward while I focused on the detection-to-GPS pipeline and integration with the rest of the system. These allocations were discussed and agreed in that first meeting; nobody was named as a single technical lead.

That was the formal picture. What actually happened was broader.

My explicit responsibility was computer vision. In practice I ended up building the full CV pipeline --- detection to GPS coordinate conversion using calibration and altitude compensation, with pitch and roll correction because the drone's attitude affects coordinate accuracy --- plus a complete simulation framework, a progressive test ladder for building confidence before each flight step, a safety-critical layer with geofence enforcement and zone repulsive fields, a web-based ground station that ran headless on the Pi, camera calibration in the lab (field of view, focal length measurements), post-processing and video analysis tools for the one real flight video we had, multiple dashboards and monitoring tools for live comparison of different vision models, and a formal complaint letter to the course organiser about the hardware situation. None of that was in my original brief.

The implicit roles accumulated one task at a time, whenever I saw a gap between where we were and a working demo. I didn't sit down and decide to take everything on; it just happened. When hardware wasn't working, I'd think ``I'll build this simulation so we can keep moving.'' When we needed to calibrate the camera, I'd think ``I'll write a calibration script.'' When Robin needed GPS coordinates for his state machine, I'd think ``I'll build the interface.'' Each task made sense individually. Collectively, they meant I was doing far more than my share, and I didn't flag that to the team early enough.

Looking back through Tuckman's (1965) developmental sequence, the role drift maps onto the Forming-to-Storming transition: individual differences in working style start showing, but the team hasn't yet confronted them openly. Robin and I were the proactive members --- when something was blocked, we'd find another angle and keep going. Edward and Zian were less proactive; they'd do what they were assigned, and do it conscientiously, but they didn't drive things forward on their own initiative. That's fair enough as a working style, but the mismatch meant the workload distribution was uneven from early on, and nobody called a meeting to address it. I certainly didn't. I just kept building.

The roles shifted in two visible ways. The first shift was slow and silent: as the two practice flight days came and went with faulty hardware (provided to us, not hardware we'd built), the project's centre of gravity migrated from the test field to my laptop. The laptop was the only place where forward progress was possible --- I could run SITL simulation while the actual drone sat in a box. Nobody declared this shift; it just accumulated. During the failed flight days, the whole team felt demotivated --- me included. I'd had loads of things prepared: an in-depth simulation I wanted to test, a bunch of test scripts I wanted to run. But instead of just being frustrated, I decided to be proactive: I'd go ahead and spend more time figuring things out in simulation, aiming for something the team could actually demonstrate. I spent 3--4$\times$ more hours than expected on this project, largely because of that decision.

The second shift was more deliberate. After the hardware issues were resolved over Easter, Robin needed detection output for his state machine. I provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. Building that interface was ``quite expensive'' --- it required attitude correction for pitch and roll, altitude compensation, and dealing with significant single-estimate error. Robin and I discussed ideas as colleagues, working in close coordination during the integration period. Robin suggested the repulsive force concept for NFZ avoidance; I thought it was a great idea, got into it, and developed it over several days of experimentation, testing edge cases like what happens when the dummy is placed very close to the no-fly zone boundary. This collaboration was the point where my role shifted from ``solo CV developer'' to ``integration partner.'' It happened late in the project, not at the start.

Drawing on Lencioni's (2002) five dysfunctions framework, which we studied in our Professional Practice sessions with Graham, I now recognise that my role drift wasn't purely conscientiousness --- it was partly an expression of the first dysfunction, \textit{absence of trust}. I was taking on work because I didn't trust that gaps would be filled otherwise. I didn't trust the process; I trusted my own output. That's a reflex I brought from my background, where individual ownership is a core value --- one person takes responsibility and delivers. I hadn't examined whether that reflex was a strength or a failure mode in a five-person team. In this project, it was both. It kept the project alive through the hardware crisis, but it also meant I was a single point of failure: the number of core modules that only I could modify was too high.

\ebi\ I should have recognised the role drift earlier and called a team meeting to renegotiate the split explicitly. The falsifiable signal I missed: if I'm taking on a task outside my original brief and can't name the teammate it unblocks, I'm probably building for myself, not for the team. That test should have been running from week one.

\subsection{1b. What I am most proud of}

I want to be honest: what I'm most proud of is the simulation work and the CV detection-to-GPS estimation pipeline.

We had extremely limited real data. One flight video from Sid --- that was it. That single video was all the real-world data available for the entire project. From that one video I had to evaluate models, test averaging techniques, deduce ground truth, retrain the detector, and calibrate the GPS estimation pipeline. Everything had to be squeezed from that one data source.

The GPS estimation problem was the core technical challenge. A single detection from a camera frame gives you a pixel coordinate. Converting that to a GPS position requires the drone's altitude, its attitude (pitch and roll), the camera's calibrated field of view, and a coordinate transform. Single estimates had significant error --- several metres off --- so I developed and tested multiple averaging techniques, each with different trade-offs. Inverse variance weighting, spatial clustering, Kalman filtering. I benchmarked all of them against ground truth I'd deduced from the video itself. This wasn't textbook work; there was no established pipeline for ``estimate a casualty's GPS position from a moving drone camera with one flight video.'' I had to figure out what would work, and the iteration cycle was: try an approach, run it against the video, measure the error, adjust, repeat.

The simulation framework was the other half. After the failed practice days --- when the hardware provided to us wasn't working and the whole team was disheartened --- I decided that sitting around feeling frustrated wasn't going to help. I'd go ahead and build a simulation detailed enough to anticipate real-world issues. I simulated GPS drift, camera shake, motion blur at different speeds, inference latency. I built a safety-critical layer with geofence enforcement and repulsive fields near the no-fly zone. I ran the simulation many times, observing closely, thinking through what could go wrong. Instead of holding problems in my head, I had a concrete environment to test against. The simulation could verify all twelve project requirements (R01--R12) end-to-end. I communicated this work to the team and they were happy with it.

\www\ The fact that everything came together on the actual demo day without prior real-hardware testing of the full pipeline says something about the simulation's quality. It was also somewhat lucky --- I'd have much preferred to run the progressive test ladder step-by-step to build genuine confidence. But the simulation was detailed enough that when the real hardware finally worked, the software running on it was already debugged.

The pride has a shadow, though. The simulation was very much my work. I spent 3--4$\times$ more hours than expected on this project, largely because of it. And that level of individual investment was also the behaviour that kept me from delegating more, from asking teammates to pair on tasks, from sharing ownership earlier. I was being productively solo at moments when the team might have benefited from a more shared response. What I'm proud of technically is also what I need to be honest about interpersonally.

\subsection{1c. What I would focus on differently in future work}

What this project forced me to confront is the distinction between \textit{capability} and \textit{enablement}. I came in believing the best engineer is the one who can fix the problem at midnight (reflection-in-action, Sch\"on, 1983). I leave believing the best engineer is the one who makes the fix legible enough that the teammate who owns the module could have found it themselves.

The evidence: when Robin needed my CV output for his state machine, I provided captured images and GPS coordinates via a defined interface. Robin then integrated without asking me questions for two days. That was the first piece of work I did on this project that scaled without my presence --- and it happened late, not early. The delay between my allocation and my first genuinely enabling contribution is the core lesson. I should have been building interfaces from week one, not accumulating private tools.

The sentence I've written in every group reflection since undergraduate is ``I will communicate better.'' It has never, once, changed my behaviour. Whatever mechanism I commit to next needs a falsifiable signal --- something I can measure after four weeks and know whether it's working or not. ``Communicate better'' isn't falsifiable. A specific practice with a specific check is.
